Chapter 1

Quarter Number Eight

The ceiling fan in Type II, Quarter No. 8 had two speeds that mattered. There was the speed it was set to, which was five, and there was the speed the voltage allowed, which in May of 1996 was something closer to two and a half. My father called the gap between them "the Electricity Board's share."

We lived on the fourth floor, the top floor, which meant that above our heads there was only the roof, and above the roof there was only the sun. By eleven in the morning the walls were warm to the touch. By two they gave off heat like a tawa left on the stove after the rotis were done. My mother hung wet bedsheets across the balcony door so that whatever wind came would come through water first. It did not help much, but it gave the flat a smell I still associate with summer: damp cotton, Lifebuoy soap, and the faint iron of the bucket.

I was eight that year. I know because that was the summer my eldest sister, Mamuni Nani, turned fourteen and announced that she was too old to sleep on the floor with the rest of us, and then slept on the floor with the rest of us anyway, because the floor was the only cool place in the flat. Four children and my mother lay on a cotton durrie in the front room like fish laid out at the Unit 1 market. My father slept on the cot near the window, with a transistor radio on his chest that he never remembered to switch off.

From the balcony you could see most of the world I knew. Directly below was the road that ran between our block and the next one, a strip of tar that went soft in the afternoons so that your chappals made a sound like a kiss when you lifted them. Beyond the last building, past the colony wall with its broken glass set into the top, was the forest. It was not called a forest. On paper it was government land, and some years later it would be cleared and turned into Janata Maidan, where politicians would hold rallies and families would come for the trade fair and eat cotton candy the colour of a nosebleed. But in 1996 it was trees, and thorn bushes, and a smell of things dying in the heat. Jackals called from it at night. Once, from the balcony, I watched four vultures sit on a dead cow for most of an afternoon, taking their time, like uncles at a wedding lunch.

On the other side, past the water tank, was more green. That was the Lab side, the research campus that everyone in the colony called RRL as if it were a word, and its trees were taller and older and darker. The older boys said a man had hanged himself there in the seventies. The older boys said a lot of things.

* * *

My father worked at Survey of India. He made maps. That is how I explained it at school, and it was true, although it took me years to understand what it really meant. He did not draw pictures of the country with crayons. He sat in an office in a building with a long verandah and checked numbers that other men had brought back from the field, numbers about heights and distances and the exact place where one district ended and another began. He had a steel scale with a bevelled edge that he used for everything, including scratching his back. He wrote the number seven with a line through it, the European way, which he had learned from some senior officer decades earlier and never given up.

He went to office on a white Narmada scooter. Everybody else's father, it seemed, had a Bajaj Chetak, and I was mildly ashamed of the Narmada until Rohan's elder brother told me it was made by a company in Gujarat that also made fertilizer, which struck both of us as the funniest thing we had ever heard. After that I was proud of it. "Our scooter is made of khaata," I told people, and some of them believed me.

The scooter lived at the bottom of the stairwell under a plastic cover that had once been a hospital mattress protector. Every morning at 9:10 my father came down the four flights with his tiffin carrier in one hand and his helmet in the other. He never wore the helmet. He hung it on the hook below the handlebar the way some men hang a lime and chillies, for protection rather than use. He would kick the scooter twice, tilt it to one side, kick it again, and then, as the engine caught, he would look up at the balcony, where I was supposed to be waving. I was almost always there.

In the evenings, around six-fifteen, you could hear the Narmada before you saw it. It had a slight wheeze when it turned off the main road into the colony, a sound like an old man clearing his throat before saying something important. If I was playing cricket in the road I would stop, bat or ball in hand, and step aside. He never stopped. He would raise two fingers from the handle, a sort of salute, and ride the last fifty metres to the stairwell. Then he would climb the stairs, slowly, because it was four floors and because he was a man who did everything at the same speed, and my mother would have tea ready at the exact moment the door opened, which I believed for years was a kind of magic and later realised was just the scooter's wheeze.

* * *

The Fifth Pay Commission came that year, or was announced, or was talked about. I did not understand it, but I understood its effect on the adults. Men stood in groups at the bottom of the stairwells after office and said numbers to each other. They said "arrears" a lot. Mr. Panda from the second floor, who was in Accounts and therefore considered an authority, explained it to anyone who would stand still. "Basic is going up. DA will be merged. You will see. It will be like a second salary."

My father listened to these conversations with his hands behind his back and his chin slightly lifted, which was his way of being polite while thinking about something else. At home he said only, "Let it come first. Then we will see." When it did come, months later, he bought a pressure cooker, a Hawkins, the big one, and a steel almirah with a mirror on the inside of the door. My mother stood in front of that mirror for a long time the evening it arrived. I don't think she was looking at herself. I think she was looking at the almirah.

That was what a government job meant in 1996, as far as I could tell. It meant the Narmada and the quarter and the almirah. It meant you would never be rich, and you would never be poor, and the number of floors you lived on was decided by your grade and not by your wishes. It meant you were safe. Every adult I knew wanted safe for their children. They wanted it so much that they talked about it constantly, the way people in a desert might talk about water.

* * *

I should explain about the colony, because nobody who did not live in one will understand it otherwise.

Survey of India Colony was not a neighbourhood. It was an organism. Every block had eight flats, two per floor, and every flat knew what every other flat was cooking by the smell in the stairwell. The Mohantys on the first floor fried fish on Wednesdays. The Raos on the third, who were from Andhra and therefore considered somewhat foreign, made something with tamarind that clung to the walls for hours. Our flat smelled, I was told, of pakhala and mustard oil, which was true of most flats, and of my mother's sandalwood agarbatti, which was not.

Nobody had a telephone. There was one in the colony office, a black thing with a lock on the dial, and you could use it for emergencies if the office was open and if Behera, the clerk, was in a good mood, which he was not, ever. So news travelled by stairwell. If you failed a test, the third floor knew before your father did. If your mother raised her voice, Mrs. Panda would bring a bowl of something over that evening and stand in the doorway asking if everything was all right, her eyes moving around the room like a ration inspector's.

My mother hated it, I think, though she would never have said so. She had grown up in a village in the west of the state, near Sambalpur, where the houses were far apart and the gossip at least had to walk to reach you. In the colony it simply came up the stairs.

"Don't tell anyone outside," she would say to us, about almost anything. What we had for dinner. How much the almirah cost. That my second sister, Lipi Nani, had got a sixty-three in mathematics. "Ghara katha ghare rahe," she said. House talk stays in the house. We were raised as small diplomats, trained to answer every question from a neighbour with a smile and nothing.

I was good at it. I was good at being quiet in general. At home with my sisters I talked constantly, but outside the flat I said very little. I had perhaps two real friends, and one of them was Rohan, and the other was a boy named Sibu who moved to Cuttack in 1997 and whom I never saw again. With other children I mostly listened. Adults found this either charming or suspicious. "So shant," Mrs. Rao would say, patting my head. "So quiet. What is going on in there?"

A great deal, as it happened. But I didn't have the words for it yet.

* * *

My sisters went to the OSAP 7th Battalion school, a government school a little way down the road towards the TV centre, and I went to Kendriya Vidyalaya No. 1 in Unit 9. This was a source of some tension, although again no one said so. KV was considered the better school. It was the central government school, the one with English-medium classes and a uniform that included a tie, and admission depended on your father's posting and transfer history and a set of rules that Mr. Panda claimed to understand. I got in. My sisters, who were older and had been enrolled when we were posted elsewhere and the rules were different, did not, or could not be moved, or something. I never got a straight answer.

So every morning I watched them leave before me. Mamuni Nani and Lipi Nani, in their blue uniforms, blue skirts and blue shirts with a white belt, hair oiled flat and plaited and tied with blue ribbons folded into flowers. They walked to school in a group with six or seven other girls from the colony, no adult, just a line of blue moving along the edge of the road with the forest on one side. They carried their bags in front of them, hugged to their chests, because someone had told them it was more ladylike. They talked the whole way. From the balcony I could hear them laughing long after I could no longer see them.

I think about that now, when I see children in cities being driven three hundred metres to school in a car with a driver and a mother and a phone. Nobody walked my sisters to school. Nobody knew where they were from eight in the morning until two in the afternoon, except that they were at school, presumably, and would come back, presumably. If one of them had fallen into a ditch, the first anyone would have known about it was when the rest of the blue line came home without her. This was not considered dangerous. This was considered Tuesday.

My youngest sister, Chiku, was four that summer. She was not yet in school, and so she was mine. She followed me everywhere I was allowed to go and several places I was not. She called me Bhai with a long rising note, "Bhaaai," as if she were calling a cow in from a field. She was the only person in the family who never compared me to anyone. I don't think it occurred to her that you could.

* * *

The heat that summer had a personality. I don't know how else to describe it. It did not just make you hot; it made you stupid, and slow, and irritable, and then it made you sleep in the afternoon whether you wanted to or not. By noon the roads of Bhubaneswar were empty. Shopkeepers pulled their shutters halfway down and lay on the counters behind them. The radio read out advisories in Odia and then in Hindi: drink water, do not go out between twelve and four, cover your head. In 1998 the heat would kill more than two thousand people across the state, and the government would declare it a natural disaster, but in 1996 it was just summer, and summer was something you survived.

My mother had rules. No going downstairs between twelve and four. A glass of water with salt and sugar every two hours. A wet cloth on the head if you complained of a headache. Onions in the pocket if you absolutely had to go out, because an onion in the pocket would absorb the loo, the hot wind, before it reached you. I believed in the onion completely. I believed in it so much that I would put one in my shorts pocket even to go to the balcony, and by evening my shorts smelled like a dhaba.

The afternoons were the worst, because I could not sit still. This is the first thing I remember knowing about myself. Everyone else could lie on the floor in the heat and be still. My sisters read, or slept, or played a game with five stones that involved throwing one in the air and scooping the others before it came down. Chiku slept with her mouth open and a line of sweat in the crease of her neck. My mother lay with her arm across her eyes. And I lay on the durrie and felt my legs.

I mean that literally. I felt them. They wanted to go. They wanted to run down four floors and across the road and along the colony wall to the gap where the bricks had fallen, and through the gap into the forest, and keep going. My legs had opinions. They itched from the inside. I would turn over, and turn over again, and press my feet against the cool of the wall, and count the blades of the fan going round, and none of it helped.

"Kana hela?" my mother would say, without moving her arm. What happened?

"Nothing."

"Then lie still."

"I am lying still."

"You are lying like a fish on a hot pan."

I would try. I would lie still for a count of a hundred, and then my left leg would kick by itself, and Lipi Nani would say "Ow," and my mother would lift her arm off her eyes and look at me, and the look said: what is wrong with this boy.

It took me about twenty-five years to answer that question. Nothing was wrong with the boy. But it is a long book, and we will get there.

* * *

In the evenings, when the sun finally gave up and slid down behind the RRL trees, the colony came back to life all at once, as if a switch had been thrown. Doors opened. Women came out on the balconies to take the dry clothes off the lines and talk across the gap between buildings. Men came home on their scooters. Somebody's radio played Vividh Bharati. Somebody's pressure cooker whistled, three times, four. And the children poured down the stairwells like water down a drain, and the road between the buildings became a cricket ground.

I would be first down. I was always first down. I would take the stairs two at a time, then three, my hand sliding along the iron railing that was still warm from the day, and burst out of the stairwell into the evening air that smelled of dust and someone's frying, and my legs would finally, finally get what they wanted.

My mother would call from the balcony: "Before dark!"

And I would shout back, "Haan, Bou!" without turning around, and we both knew that "before dark" meant "roughly when the jackals start."

Nobody could call me home. There was no way to. Once I was down those stairs, I belonged to the road and the forest and whatever Rohan had decided we were doing that evening, and the only thing that would bring me back was the dark, or hunger, or the wheeze of a Narmada turning in from the main road at six-fifteen.

That was the world. Four floors, a road, a forest, a scooter. It was small. I didn't know yet how small. I only knew that I had too much in me for the afternoon, and that the evening was the only time I fit.

Chapter 2

The Bowler

Rohan Patnaik lived in Quarter No. 14, ground floor, which was the best position in the whole colony for cricket purposes. His bedroom window opened straight onto the road, so if the ball went in, it was his mother who threw it back out, and his mother had a good arm.

He was a batsman. He had been born a batsman, the way some people are born left-handed. He had a stance before he had a bat. When he was six he stood at his front door with a rolled-up newspaper and took guard against an imaginary Wasim Akram, tapping the newspaper on the floor twice and then lifting it high behind his head, exactly like Sachin, and his father took a photo of it with a Hot Shot camera that the whole colony later saw, because his mother had it framed. Rohan liked the photo. He thought it showed promise.

I bowled.

Our pitch was the road itself, from the electric pole outside Block C to a line we had scratched on the tar with a stone. The wickets were three bricks stacked on top of each other at the batting end and one of my father's old Survey of India file boxes at the bowler's end, filled with sand so it wouldn't blow away. The boundary was the drain on one side and the colony wall on the other. Hitting it over the wall into the forest was out, because someone had to go and get it, and nobody wanted to go and get it, because of the snakes. Hitting a balcony was four. Hitting Mrs. Panda's balcony was out, and you had to go upstairs and apologise, which was worse than any snake.

We played with a tennis ball when we had one and a rubber ball when we didn't. In 1997, for one glorious month, we had a real leather ball that Rohan's uncle had brought from Calcutta, and we played with it exactly four times before Lipi Nani's friend got hit on the shin and her mother came to every flat in the block, one by one, and the ball was confiscated by a committee of mothers and never seen again. I believe it is still in an almirah somewhere in Survey of India Colony, wrapped in a petticoat.

* * *

There were usually six or seven of us. Rohan. Me. Sibu, until he moved. The two Rao brothers, who argued in Telugu when they disagreed with a decision and in Hindi when they wanted everyone to hear. A small furious boy called Tukuna from Block A who could not bat or bowl but could field like a dog chasing a bus. And sometimes Pintu, who was thirteen and therefore too old for us, but who played anyway when the older boys wouldn't have him.

Everyone wanted to bat. This is the law of street cricket everywhere in India and probably the world. Batting is where you stand still and become famous. Bowling is where you run and run and run and somebody else becomes famous off your work. When we picked teams, there was always a long negotiation about the batting order, with appeals, counter-appeals, threats to take the bat home, and finally a compromise that satisfied nobody.

I never joined the negotiation. From the first time we played, I said the same thing.

"I'll bowl. You keep batting."

Rohan looked at me the first time I said it as if I had offered him my lunch money. "Pura over?"

"All of them. I'll do most of the bowling. You bat."

"You're mad."

"I like it."

"Nobody likes bowling, re. You run all the way there, you throw, and then I hit it, and then you have to go get it." He gestured down the road, towards the drain and the place where the road curved and the ball always ended up under somebody's scooter. "And then you walk all the way back. It's like being a servant."

"I like it," I said again, and went to mark my run-up.

He thought I was stupid. He said so, frequently, over the next ten years. "Saala, you take the donkey job every time." But he let me do it, because it meant he got to bat, and Rohan would have let the devil bowl to him if it meant he got to bat.

* * *

I want to be honest about why I did it, because for a long time I wasn't sure myself, and when I was finally sure, the answer surprised me.

Part of it was my legs. Bowling used them. Batting, in our road, was mostly standing at the bricks waiting for someone to deliver the ball, and I had enough standing still in the afternoons. Bowling meant twelve long strides from the file box, a jump, a whirl of the arm, a follow-through that took me halfway down the pitch, and then a sprint wherever the ball had gone. By the end of an evening I would be dripping, my vest stuck to my back, my legs finally quiet. I slept well on bowling nights.

But part of it was something else, something that I didn't have words for at eight and only half had at twelve. It was this: I knew I was not going to be the best at anything by avoiding the hard part. Nobody told me this. I worked it out from watching. The boys who only batted got good at batting in our road, against our bowling, which was not very good, and then the moment they played against older boys or in the school ground, they were out in two balls and came back sulking. The boys who bowled, the few who did, got stronger. Their shoulders got stronger, their legs, their lungs. They could run all evening. You could see it in them.

I didn't want to be famous in our road. I wanted to be strong. I had a body that was quick but light, wiry, the kind of body aunties described as "lean, but he eats," and I knew, the way you know things at eight without being able to explain them, that the only way it was going to change was if I gave it the hard job, every day, whether anyone noticed or not.

Nobody noticed. That was fine. That was, I think, the point.

* * *

After cricket, in the twenty minutes between the end of the game and the arrival of the jackals, we sat on the low wall outside Block C and traded cards.

Center Fresh was the chewing gum with the liquid in the middle, the one that squirted when you bit it, and in 1997 it came with cricket cards. Each card had a player's photo on the front, a little stiff and over-saturated, and on the back his statistics: matches, runs, wickets, highest score, best bowling. There were nearly two hundred of them in the set. We bought the gum for the cards and chewed it out of a sense of duty. Most of the gum ended up stuck under the wall, a grey archaeological layer that is probably still there.

Rohan had the best collection because his father gave him two rupees every day. I had a smaller collection but a better system. I kept mine in a Gold Flake cigarette tin my father had found in a field office in Koraput years before, and I sorted them by country and then by role. Batsmen at the front. Bowlers at the back. All-rounders in the middle, where they could go either way.

"Sachin," Rohan would say, holding up a card like a priest with a lamp. "Best batsman in the world."

"Lara," said the elder Rao brother, who supported the West Indies to annoy everyone.

"Lara is nothing in front of Sachin."

"Lara made 375."

"Sachin will make 400."

"When?"

"Soon."

And so on. There was no question of who was the best batsman. It was Sachin, it had always been Sachin, and the only people who said otherwise were people who wanted a fight. The real arguments were about bowling.

"Wasim," I said, every time.

"Wasim is Pakistani."

"So? He's the best."

"Srinath is faster."

"Wasim swings it both ways. Srinath only goes straight." I had no idea if this was true. I had never seen Srinath bowl except on the small Onida television in the Pandas' flat, through a fog of static, during a match whose result I have forgotten. But I had read the backs of the cards so many times that I could recite their figures like the times tables, and I knew that bowling was harder, and I knew that whoever was best at the harder thing deserved more respect.

"Bowlers don't win matches," said Rohan.

"Bowlers are the only ones who can win matches. A batsman can only make runs. A bowler can end the other team."

That shut him up for once. He looked at me with an expression I would come to know very well, a mix of irritation and reluctant interest, the face of someone who has just heard you say something they will think about later and never admit to.

* * *

We also had WWE cards, although we called it WWF then, and nobody knew anything about wrestling except what we saw in the cards and what a boy in Block F whose family had cable told us. Cable was an elite thing in the colony in those years. A handful of flats had it, wires slung from balcony to balcony like washing lines, and the rest of us had Doordarshan and an antenna on the roof that had to be turned by hand whenever a crow sat on it. The boy in Block F was called Bapi and his father was something important in the Accounts section, and he was the most popular boy in the colony for exactly as long as his family had cable, which was about eighteen months, after which his father was transferred to Guwahati and his authority went with him.

Bapi told us about the Undertaker, who could not die, and about a man called Stone Cold who drank beer on television. These things seemed impossible and therefore true. I had a card of the Undertaker that I kept separately, in my shirt pocket, for luck. It went through the wash twice and came out looking like a ghost of a ghost, which I thought was appropriate.

* * *

In the flat, each of us had a slab. This was a cement shelf built into the wall of the bedroom, the kind every government quarter had, a grey ledge about a metre long with a lip at the front. Four slabs, four children, one each. Mamuni Nani's was neat, with her books in order of height and a small plastic Ganesh at the end. Lipi Nani's was a mess. Chiku's was mostly a place where other people put things they didn't want, until she was old enough to object.

Mine was a shrine.

I cut cricketers out of everything. The Sportstar that Rohan's father bought, when Rohan had finished with it. The Sunday supplement of the Samaja, which carried sports photos so blurred that you had to guess who it was from the shape of the moustache. The back of a Bournvita tin that had a picture of Sachin with a glass of milk. I cut them out with my mother's tailoring scissors, which I was not supposed to touch, and stuck them to the wall above my slab with rice paste that my mother made for me, under protest, every few weeks. "If the landlord comes and sees this," she said, "we will have to pay."

"We don't have a landlord, Bou. It's government."

"Then the government will come and see this."

The government never came. By the end of 1998 the wall above my slab was a collage of Indian cricket, overlapping, edges curling, Azhar's wrists and Kumble's glasses and Sachin, always Sachin, in six or seven different poses, in the middle of it all. At the very edge, in the corner, half covered by a picture of Ajay Jadeja, was a small photo of Wasim Akram mid-delivery that I had cut from somebody's Pakistani magazine at the barber's. I kept it near the corner because I was not entirely sure my father would approve. He never mentioned it. I think he saw it on the first day.

* * *

There is one evening I remember more clearly than the others, though nothing much happened in it.

It was late in the summer of 1997, a week or two before the rains, the sky gone the colour of dal and the air so thick you could have spread it on bread. The others had gone home early because Tukuna's mother had come out and dragged him away by the ear, and that had somehow ended the game for everyone. Rohan went in to take his bath. The road was empty.

I stayed.

I put the bricks back up at the batting end. I took the tennis ball, which was going bald on one side, and walked back to the file box, and marked my run-up again with my heel. And then I bowled. At nothing. At three bricks in an empty road. I ran in, jumped, bowled, followed through, walked down the pitch, picked up the ball from wherever it had gone, walked back, and did it again.

I don't know how long I did it. It got dark enough that I couldn't see the bricks properly and had to aim for the pale square they made against the tar. The lights came on in the stairwells. Someone's radio was playing an old Odia song, Akshaya Mohanty, I think, the voice coming down through the evening like a slow kite. I bowled, and walked, and bowled. Nobody was watching. I wasn't practising for any match. There was no match.

I hit the bricks, finally, after what must have been fifty balls. The top one fell off with a small clack that sounded enormous in the empty road. I stood there in my follow-through, breathing hard, and looked at it.

Then I heard my mother's voice from four floors up. "Max! Andhaara hela! Upare aa!" It's dark. Come up.

I picked up the brick and put it back on the stack. I picked up the ball. I went up the four floors at a walk, for once, my legs heavy and happy.

At the top of the stairs, my father was standing in the doorway, in his vest and lungi, a glass of tea in his hand. I don't know how long he had been there. Our balcony looked straight down on the road. He might have watched the whole thing.

"Hit it?" he said.

"Once."

He nodded, as if this were a perfectly reasonable answer, and stood aside to let me in. He did not say well done, or what were you doing, or why do you waste your time throwing a ball at bricks in the dark. He just nodded. It was the nod he gave to a number when it checked out.

I didn't know then how long I would carry that nod. I'm still carrying it.

Chapter 3

Brown Paper

Before the tree, there were the books, and before the books, there was the brown paper.

Every year, in the last week of June, my mother bought a roll of it from the stationery shop near the Unit 9 market, along with a packet of name labels, a bottle of Fevicol and, if it had been a good month, a strip of clear plastic that she would not let any of us touch until she had measured it twice. Then, on a Sunday afternoon, with the first rain falling outside and the whole flat smelling of wet earth through the open balcony door, the five of us sat on the floor and covered books.

This was a serious business. A KV textbook had to be covered so that the corners were sharp and the spine was tight and nothing bulged. You laid the book open on the paper, cut around it with two fingers of extra, folded the edges in, creased them with your thumbnail, and then did a thing at the corners, a sort of envelope fold, that my mother could do in one movement and I could not do at all. Mamuni Nani was nearly as good as her. Lipi Nani was quick and sloppy. Chiku, at five, was in charge of handing out the labels and licking them, which she did with tremendous concentration and too much tongue.

"Not like that." My mother took my book from me, unfolded my corner, and folded it again so that it lay flat as a blade. "If it is not neat, the teacher will think you are not neat."

"But I'm not neat."

"Then at least the book should be."

And then the label. A small white rectangle with a blue border and lines on it. Name. Class. Section. Subject. School. I wrote my name in my best handwriting, which was not very good, and then wrote CLASS IV-B, and then KENDRIYA VIDYALAYA NO. 1, BHUBANESWAR, and the letters got smaller and smaller towards the end until NESWAR was squashed against the border like passengers on a city bus.

The smell of a new textbook is a smell I would know in my sleep. It is a little like a ration shop, a little like a photocopier, and a little like something that has never been used, which is the hardest smell to describe because it is mostly the absence of other smells. I used to open each book in the middle and put my nose right into the fold and breathe in. Lipi Nani said it was disgusting. Then she did it too, when she thought nobody was looking.

* * *

Kendriya Vidyalaya No. 1 was on the other side of the Unit 9 market, behind the Ram Temple. You heard the temple before you saw the school. In the mornings there would be bells, and the smell of camphor and marigolds, and a loudspeaker playing bhajans slightly too loud and slightly out of tune, and then you would turn the corner and there would be the gate, and the long buildings with their red-oxide floors, and the ground where we had assembly in rows by height.

I was always near the back. Not because I was tall, though I was taller than average, but because I had learned that the back row of assembly was the safest place in the world. Nobody looks at the back row. You can move your feet in the back row, shift your weight, flex your toes inside your shoes, and no one sees.

Assembly was in Hindi and English. We sang "Hum Honge Kamyab" and a prayer whose words I knew perfectly and whose meaning I never once thought about. A senior read the news headlines from the paper. Someone from Class XII stood on a platform and said, "Thought for the day," and then said something about honesty or hard work, and we all clapped without listening. Then we marched to our classrooms in lines, past the noticeboard where the toppers' names were pinned, which I always looked at and never found myself on.

Going to KV in those years meant something. It meant your father was a central government employee. It meant you would wear a tie, a grey and blue striped one with an elastic band at the back, which made you look, to the boys in the OSAP school down the road, like a small bank manager. It meant English, more or less. It meant that if your father was transferred to Shillong or Jammu or Port Blair, there would be a KV there with the same syllabus and the same textbooks and probably the same tie, and you would sit down on the same wooden bench and pick up where you left off. My father liked that. It was the education equivalent of the steel almirah. It was safe.

The benches were wooden, long, made for two but usually holding three. They were scarred with the initials of every child who had sat there since independence. RK + SM. AJAY IS A DONKEY. A small carefully carved map of India that someone had spent what must have been weeks on, with Kashmir slightly too large. You were not supposed to write on the benches, and everybody did, with compass points and the backs of earrings and once, memorably, with a geometry divider that Tukuna's cousin had heated over a candle in the chemistry lab.

I sat on the third bench from the window. From there, if I turned my head slightly, I could see a neem tree outside and a slice of the sky, and if I turned it a little more, I could see the corner of the ground, which in the monsoon filled with water and frogs. I turned my head a lot. Teachers wrote it in my report card in several different ways over the years. "Attention wanders." "Must concentrate." "Is often looking outside." One of them, in Class V, wrote simply "Dreamer," which my father read out loud at the dinner table in a tone that suggested it was not a compliment.

* * *

The gel pen craze arrived in about 1998, and it hit us like a fever.

They came from somewhere, nobody knew where, maybe Delhi, maybe Calcutta, maybe Japan. They had transparent bodies and rubber grips and ink in colours that did not exist in nature: silver, gold, a pink that was almost orange, a green you could see in the dark. They cost ten or twelve rupees each, which was a fortune. You could not write your answers in them, because teachers only allowed blue or black, and anyway the ink ran out in about a week. They were completely useless. Everybody had to have one.

Rohan got the silver one first. He wrote his name on the back of his hand with it and showed everyone on the bus, holding his hand up like a man showing a wound. Then a girl in Section A got a set of ten in a plastic case, and that was the end of Rohan's reign.

I saved for a month. I gave up Center Fresh, which meant giving up cards, which was a real sacrifice. I bought a single gel pen in a sort of deep blue that had glitter in it, and I wrote, in very small letters, on the inside back cover of my Hindi notebook, where no teacher would ever look, the names of the eleven players I would pick for India. Then I wrote them again in a different order. Then I wrote my own name at the bottom, number twelve, the extra fielder, and looked at it for a while, and then I put the cap back on the pen.

The pen ran out in nine days. I kept it anyway, empty, in my pencil box, for years.

* * *

The tree happened in Class IV, in the second week of July 1997.

Our environmental studies teacher was a woman I will call Mrs. Das, because that is not her name and because she may still be alive and I have no wish to hurt her. She was tall and wore her sari pinned very tightly, and she had a way of holding a piece of chalk between two fingers like a cigarette. She was not a cruel woman, I think. She was a tired one. She had forty-four children in a room built for thirty and a syllabus that had to be finished by March, and she did not have time for anything that slowed her down.

That day the lesson was about trees. Why trees are important. Oxygen, shade, soil, rain, birds. Van Mahotsav, the tree-planting festival, which was coming up in the first week of July, and which we had already missed, but never mind. The school was going to plant saplings along the boundary wall, and each section would get two.

"So," said Mrs. Das, tapping the chalk on the board where she had written TREE PLANTATION in capital letters, "when is the best time to plant a tree?"

Hands went up. I don't know why mine did. I almost never put my hand up. But I had been thinking about this exact question, not because of the lesson but because of the balcony.

The year before, Chiku and I had planted a mango seed in a Dalda tin on the balcony. It was June, just before the rains. We put soil in the tin from the bottom of the stairwell and pushed the seed in and watered it once, and then the rains came, and we forgot about it, and it grew. It grew by itself. By August it was a little bright green thing with two leaves the colour of copper. We were very proud. Then in October the rains stopped, and we were still not watering it, because we had never had to, and it died. One morning it was just a brown stick in a tin.

I had thought about this a lot. I had decided that the rain had cheated us. The rain had made the plant think everything was easy, and it had made us think everything was easy, and when the rain went away neither of us knew what to do.

Mrs. Das pointed at the girl in the front row, who said, "Ma'am, monsoon, Ma'am."

"Correct. Why?"

"Because of rain, Ma'am. The soil is wet."

"Correct. Good." She turned to write MONSOON on the board, and then she saw my hand still up. "Yes?"

I stood up, because at KV you stood up to answer.

"Ma'am, I think we should plant in summer."

There was a pause. I could feel the class turning, forty-three heads, the scrape of a bench.

"In summer," said Mrs. Das.

"Yes, Ma'am. Because in the monsoon everything grows anyway. Even without us. Grass grows, weeds grow, everything. So it's not really planting. The rain is doing it." My face was getting hot. I had not planned to say this much. "But if we plant in summer, and we water it every day, then it's really ours. And it will be strong, because it learned when it was hard. And then when the rain comes it will just..." I didn't know the word. "It will just be extra."

There was another pause, longer this time.

Mrs. Das put down her chalk.

"Are you stupid?"

She didn't shout it. That was the thing. She said it in an ordinary voice, almost curious, the way you might ask someone if they had forgotten their umbrella.

"Why would anyone plant a tree in summer, when it is forty-five degrees outside, and you have to carry water every single day, when you can just wait one month and plant it in the monsoon and God will water it for you? Hm? Use your brain. Sit down."

Somebody laughed. Then several people laughed. Tukuna's cousin, from the bench behind me, said "Stupid" under his breath, in a delighted mutter, like a boy who has found a coin.

I sat down.

* * *

I didn't cry. I want to say that, because for a long time afterwards I was ashamed that I had wanted to, and I think it matters that I didn't. I sat down and looked at my brown paper cover and the label with my squashed name on it, and I traced the edge of the label with my fingernail, around and around, until the bell went.

What I felt was not exactly hurt. It was something stranger. It was as if I had held out a thing I had made, something I had been carrying for a whole year, and someone had looked at it and not seen anything there. Not a bad thing. Nothing. As if I had held out my empty hand.

Because I knew I was right. That was the part I could not get past. I knew she was right too; I wasn't a complete fool, I understood that monsoon planting was easier and more sensible and what everyone did. But I also knew that what I meant was true, and that she had not heard what I meant. She had heard a boy say "summer" and stopped listening at the word.

I walked home with Rohan that afternoon and didn't say anything. He talked about a Center Fresh card he had got that morning, a Robin Singh, which he wanted to trade, and I said "Hmm" and "Accha" in the right places, and when we got to the colony I went up the four floors and didn't come back down for cricket.

"Kana hela?" said my mother, when I sat on the balcony with my knees up.

"Nothing."

"Fever?" She put the back of her hand on my forehead.

"No."

She looked at me for a while, and then she went back inside, and I heard her say something to Mamuni Nani in a low voice, and Mamuni Nani said, "How do I know? Ask him."

Nobody asked me.

* * *

That night, after the others were asleep on the floor, I took a notebook from my slab. It was a thin one, the cheap kind with a picture of a film actress on the cover that the market shops sold for two rupees, and I had bought it for rough work and never used it. I took out the dead gel pen from my pencil box and remembered it was dead, and took out an ordinary Reynolds instead. I went out on the balcony where the light from the stairwell came in yellow through the grille, and I sat on the floor with my back against the wall, and I opened the notebook to the first page.

I didn't know what I was going to write. I don't think I had ever written anything that wasn't for school. I sat there with the pen for a long time, while the jackals started up in the forest and a scooter went past on the main road and the night went quiet in the way it only does at the top of a building.

Then I wrote, in letters that sloped down to the right:

I am not stupid. I just think about it differently.

I looked at it. It looked like something written by somebody else. I underlined it twice.

Then, because it seemed wrong to leave it alone on the page, I wrote underneath, smaller: Mango tree died in October. Next time water it.

I closed the notebook and put it under my slab, behind the textbooks in their brown paper, where nobody would find it.

The school planted its saplings along the boundary wall that week, in the rain, as planned. Section B got a gulmohar and a neem. I watched the peon dig the holes. By the next summer, the gulmohar was dead and the neem was the height of my knee, and the peon used to water it out of a bucket in May, grumbling, every afternoon at three.

Chapter 4

When It Rained

In Bhubaneswar the rain did not visit. It moved in.

People who grew up in cities where it rains for an hour in the afternoon and then politely stops will not understand this. In Odisha the monsoon came off the Bay of Bengal in great slow systems that sat on top of the state like a hen on eggs. When it started, it kept going. Two days, three days, sometimes four. The sky went the colour of a slate in the first hour and stayed that colour until you forgot the sun had ever existed. The drops were enormous. They hit the balcony railing like small stones and burst, and the sound on the roof above us, directly above us, because we were on the top floor and there was only one slab of concrete between my head and God, was like being inside a drum that somebody was beating with both fists.

I loved it for the first day. Everyone did. The heat broke all at once, like a fever, and the whole colony came out onto their balconies to watch. The smell came up from the earth, that smell, the one that has a scientific name I would learn years later in a college lecture hall and immediately forget because the name was so much smaller than the smell. Children ran into the road in their vests and stood with their mouths open. Mothers shouted at them to come in and then stood watching them with their own faces turned up.

By the second day I was nervous. By the third I was frightened.

* * *

The thing I was frightened of was drowning. Not me, personally. The building. I had seen a programme on Doordarshan, a documentary or maybe a news report, about floods somewhere, Bangladesh perhaps, or Bihar, with people sitting on the roofs of their houses with water all the way up to the gutters, and a cow floating past on its side. I had seen it once, for perhaps a minute, and it had lodged in me like a splinter.

From our balcony, after three days of rain, the road below was a river. Not a deep one. It came up to the knees of the men who waded through it with their trousers rolled and their shoes in their hands, but it moved, brown and fast, carrying leaves and plastic and once a whole slipper, which I watched sail past with great seriousness, like a ship going to war. Water poured off the edge of our roof in a sheet. Water came out of the drainpipes in arcs. Water ran down the inside wall of the stairwell where a crack had opened years before and never been fixed, and collected on the landings, and Mrs. Panda put a bucket under it and then complained to my mother that the bucket was always full.

I lay on the floor at night and listened to it and did sums. The road was one foot deep. If it rained for one more day, two feet. The ground floor was about three feet above the road. Then the first floor. Then the second. I worked out, using a method that had nothing to do with science and everything to do with fear, that we had perhaps nine days before the water reached the fourth floor.

"Bou," I said into the dark. "What if it doesn't stop?"

"It will stop."

"But what if it doesn't?"

My mother sighed. She was lying on her back with Chiku tucked into her side. "Then we will go to the roof."

"And then?"

"And then we will wait for the Survey of India to come and measure it."

Lipi Nani laughed out loud. My mother laughed too, quietly, and after a moment I did, and the laugh took some of the splinter out. But I still lay awake for a long time, listening to the water on the roof, and in the morning, before anyone else was up, I went out onto the balcony to check the road. It was still a river. It was not deeper. I measured it against the steps of Block D and wrote the number in my diary: Day 4. Third step. Same as yesterday.

That was the second thing I ever wrote in it. I think it is the first time I used it to hold something I could not hold myself.

* * *

The rains turned the forest into something else.

On dry days, the forest beyond the colony wall was thorny and grey and full of rustling. In the monsoon it went green so fast you could almost watch it. Creepers came up over the wall. The empty colony playground, the one that nobody maintained, filled up with grass as high as my chest, and the cricket moved permanently to the road. Frogs came out in hundreds, and the sound of them at night was so loud that it sometimes drowned the rain. Snakes came out too, looking for dry ground, and every monsoon someone in the colony found one somewhere it should not be. In 1997 it was a rat snake in the Raos' bathroom, which Mr. Rao killed with a broom while Mrs. Rao stood on the dining table and shrieked in Telugu. In 1998 it was a krait in the coal shed behind Block B, and nobody killed that one. They called a man from somewhere in Saheed Nagar who came with a stick and a sack, and the entire colony stood in a wide circle and watched him, and he picked it up as if it were a dupatta and dropped it in the sack and went away without a word, and for a week afterwards every boy in the colony walked around with a stick.

Rohan and I used to go to the gap in the colony wall in the evenings, when the rain eased, and look through. We never went in during the monsoon. We were brave but we were not stupid. We just looked. The trees dripped. Mist lay low on the ground between them. Sometimes, far in, you could see a pair of eyes, low down, catching the last light. Jackals, we told each other. Always jackals.

"One day I'm going to go all the way through," Rohan said once. "To the other side."

"What's on the other side?"

"The TV centre."

"You can go to the TV centre on the road."

"That's not the point, re."

I knew it wasn't. That was what I liked about him. Rohan understood that there were things you did because they were the hard way, he just didn't understand it about bowling.

* * *

The monsoon was also the season of Doordarshan.

We did not have cable. We had a black and white Weston television in a wooden cabinet with sliding doors that my father had bought before I was born, and in 1997 we got a colour one, a BPL, on instalments, which was the second-biggest event of the year after the almirah. The antenna was on the roof, tied to the water tank with wire, and in the rain it swung, and whenever it swung the picture on the BPL turned to snow and the sound turned to a roar like the sea. Then someone had to go up to the roof and turn it. That someone was me.

"Max! Antenna!"

I would go up the last flight of stairs to the roof door, which had a padlock that was never locked, and out into the rain, and grab the cold wet pole and turn it a few degrees, and shout down the stairwell, "Now?"

From inside, faintly: "No!"

Turn. "Now?"

"Little more!"

Turn. "Now?"

"Haan, haan, haan! Stop! Don't touch it!"

And I would stand there in the rain, soaked through, holding the pole absolutely still, while three floors below my family watched television. Sometimes I stood there for the whole of a programme. Once, during a cricket match, I stood there for most of an innings, and Lipi Nani brought me an umbrella and a glass of tea and stood with me, and we listened to the commentary coming up faintly through the window of the Pandas' flat below, and that was one of the best afternoons of my childhood. We didn't even see the match.

The programmes I remember are not always the ones I should. There was Shaktimaan, of course, which started in September 1997 on Sunday afternoons and which every child in India watched in total silence, as if in church. Shaktimaan was a man in a red and gold costume who spun around very fast and then flew, and at the end of each episode he gave a little speech about being a good person, about not telling lies, about saying sorry, and we listened to these speeches with an attention we never gave our teachers. Some children in the country jumped off buildings thinking Shaktimaan would save them, and there were reports in the papers, and after that the show added a warning. My mother read me the report from the Samaja and made me promise that I would never jump off the balcony. I was insulted. I was nine. I knew he was an actor. I also, secretly, was not completely sure.

And then there were the English shows, the strange ones that appeared on Doordarshan in the late afternoons with no explanation and no dubbing, as if somebody in Delhi had found a box of tapes and decided to play them. A cartoonish thing called Superhuman Samurai Syber-Squad, about an American teenager who went inside a computer and fought monsters, which I understood perhaps one sentence of in four and loved completely. A show about an American family where a boy called Cory had a teacher who lived next door, and the teacher was always right, and the boy was always learning something. Boy Meets World. I didn't know what half the words meant, but I knew the shape of it. It was about a boy who kept getting things wrong and a grown-up who kept believing he would get them right anyway.

I watched that show with a feeling I could not name. Years later I would recognise it as envy.

* * *

School did not stop for rain. KV did not believe in rain. On the third day of a downpour we would still put on our uniforms, still knot our elastic ties, still carry our brown-papered books in plastic bags to keep them dry, and still wade to the bus stop at the end of the colony road with our shoes in our hands. The school bus, when it came, was full of wet children and smelled of wet children, which is a smell like a dog that has been reading.

At the gate, after school, there were the vendors. They were there in rain and heat and anything else, under polythene sheets tied between bamboo poles. There was the samosa man, whose samosas were small and triangular and so hot inside that the first bite was a gamble every time. There was a man who sold boiled chana from a big aluminium pot with chopped onions and green chilli and a squeeze of lemon, in a cone of newspaper that went soggy before you were halfway through. There was the jhal muri man, who could make a paper cone with one hand.

And there was the Pepsi man.

He did not sell Pepsi. That is, he did not sell the drink that came in a glass bottle with a blue label. What he sold, and what every child in Bhubaneswar called Pepsi, was a long thin tube of plastic filled with coloured sugar water and frozen solid in an icebox on the back of his bicycle. Orange, red, green, a kind of brown that was supposed to be cola. Fifty paise each, or one rupee for the long ones. You bit the end off and sucked, and the colour came out first and the ice stayed behind, so that by the end you were holding a tube of nearly white ice and your tongue was the colour of a traffic signal. We ate them in the rain. We ate them in May at forty-four degrees, when they melted faster than you could suck and ran down your wrist to your elbow. My mother said they were made with tap water and would give us typhoid. They probably were. None of us got typhoid, or if we did, we blamed something else.

The real Pepsi, the glass-bottled one, was a treat for weddings and guests. When an uncle visited, my father would send me down to the shop at the corner of the main road with a cloth bag and the exact money, and I would come back up four floors very slowly, holding the bag in front of me with both hands, the bottles clinking against each other. Pepsi, Coca-Cola, and an orange one, Mirinda, which Chiku called Miranda, and which we all called Miranda after that, until the name stuck so hard that years later I was genuinely surprised to see it spelled correctly on a can.

You opened them with the opener that hung on a string from a nail in the kitchen. The cap came off with a little gasp, and a breath of cold smoke came out of the neck, and then you drank, and the fizz was so strong in those years that it went straight up the back of your nose and made your eyes water. Lipi Nani claimed that once, during a laughing fit at a cousin's wedding, Mirinda had come out of her nose. Nobody believed her, but we asked her to tell the story at every wedding afterwards, and she always did, and every time it got more orange.

* * *

One night in August 1998, during a long rain, the power went off at about nine, which was normal, and did not come back, which was less normal. My father lit the kerosene lantern and hung it from the hook in the front room, and its light made everything gold and shadowy and enormous. The rain on the roof was so loud that we had to speak up to hear each other. Chiku was frightened and sat on my mother's lap. Mamuni Nani was studying for her Class X exams and kept reading by the lantern, her lips moving, as if the rain were just a kind of silence.

I sat on the balcony with my diary.

I could not see the road. I could barely see the next building. The lantern behind me threw my shadow out through the grille and into the rain, a long thin boy, broken into squares. I wrote, with the notebook almost on my nose to see: Day 3. Can't see the step. Bapa says the drain will take it. Rain is not angry, it is just a lot.

I don't know where I got that last line. I think my father said something like it, earlier, without meaning anything by it. I wrote it down because I liked it. I liked that you could be a lot without being angry.

Behind me, my father said, "Max. Come inside. You'll get wet."

"I'm already wet."

There was a pause, and then he came out, in his vest, and stood beside me at the grille with his hands behind his back, looking at the rain he could not see. He did not say anything for a long time. Then he said, "When I was young, in the village, we used to sit out in this. On the verandah. My father used to say it is the only time the land gets to drink properly."

"Didn't you get scared?"

"Of rain?"

"Of it not stopping."

He thought about this seriously, the way he thought about everything. "It always stops," he said. "That is the one thing I know about rain. The question is only what you have done before it stops."

I didn't understand that for a long time. I wrote it down anyway.

Chapter 5

The Bus West

We left for Mamu's village every year on the second day of the summer holidays, never the first. The first day, my mother said, was for washing.

She washed everything. Every bedsheet, every curtain, every towel, every shirt we owned and several we didn't, which had been left behind by cousins the previous year. By evening the balcony and the roof were draped like a ship under sail, and the flat smelled so strongly of Nirma that Chiku sneezed every time she went past the bathroom. Then she ironed what needed ironing, with the heavy iron that had to be heated on the gas, and folded everything into two tin trunks and a VIP suitcase with a broken wheel, and sat on the suitcase to close it while my father did the latch.

"We are going for three weeks," my father said, watching. "Not three years."

"In the village there is no Nirma."

"There is Nirma in the village. I have seen it."

"Not the right Nirma."

He gave up. He always gave up on Nirma.

* * *

The bus left from the Baramunda bus stand at six in the morning, which meant leaving the flat at five-fifteen, in the dark, in a cycle rickshaw piled so high with luggage that the rickshaw-wallah had to stand on the pedals to get it moving. My father rode behind on the Narmada with Lipi Nani on the back, holding the suitcase across her lap. The rest of us were in the rickshaw, and the rickshaw-wallah sang to himself, an old Sambalpuri song, I think, because he had found out where we were going and decided to be friendly.

The bus stand at that hour was its own country. Buses with names painted on their foreheads in curly letters: Maa Samaleswari, Jay Jagannath, Bhagaban, Laxmi Travels. Men on the roofs throwing luggage up and tying it down with rope. A tea stall with an enormous kettle that never stopped boiling. Conductors shouting destinations like auctioneers: "Sambalpur-Bargarh-Sambalpur-Bargarh!" and "Angul-Angul-Angul!" so that the place names lost their meaning and became chants.

My father always booked seats in advance, which was rare and slightly embarrassing, because the conductor had to clear them for us and people who had been sitting there gave us looks. "We have small children," my father said, though by then only Chiku was small, and he said it in the voice of a man reading out a government circular, and nobody argued.

I always got the window. I don't remember ever asking for it. It was just understood that Max had to be next to a window or he would be climbing out of one.

* * *

The journey to the west was eight or nine hours, depending on the road and the driver and how many times the bus stopped to let somebody off at a crossing in the middle of nowhere, where there was nothing but a tea stall and a banyan tree and a dirt track disappearing into fields.

The road went through everything. First the edges of Bhubaneswar, the new colonies, half-built houses with iron rods sticking out of their roofs like hair. Then Cuttack, briefly, the river wide and brown and low because it was summer. Then the long road through Dhenkanal and Angul, where the land changed. The paddy fields thinned out. The ground turned red and stony. Hills appeared, low and blue in the distance, and forest came down to the road in places, sal trees with their big leaves and their straight grey trunks, and once, I swear it, an elephant, far off, standing in a clearing, though nobody else on the bus saw it and Lipi Nani said it was a rock.

"It moved," I said.

"Rocks don't move."

"This one did."

"Then it was a moving rock."

It was hot. It was always hot. This was April and May in Odisha, and the bus was a metal box with windows that were either jammed open or jammed shut. The wind that came in through the open ones was like the breath of a cooker. My mother tied a wet handkerchief around Chiku's head, and then around her own, and then, after a long negotiation, around mine. The loo blew in red dust that settled on everything, on our clothes, our hair, our eyebrows, the bananas my mother had packed in a cloth bag, so that by Angul we were all a slightly different colour.

In 1998, the summer the heat killed all those people, we made the trip anyway. I remember the bus stopping in a town I don't know the name of, around noon, and the driver getting out and simply lying down in the shade under the bus. Nobody argued. The conductor lay down next to him. Some of the passengers got down and lay down too, and for an hour the bus sat there on the side of the road with its engine off and fifteen people sleeping underneath it like puppies under a cart. My father did not lie down. He stood in the shade of a tamarind tree with his hands behind his back, looking at the road ahead, and he did not look worried, exactly, but he did look like a man who was checking something.

* * *

I could not sit still on the bus. Of course I could not. Nine hours. My legs had complaints by the end of the first hour and formal grievances by the third. I shifted. I swung my feet. I put my knees up against the seat in front, until the man sitting there turned around. I counted electric poles. I counted cows, then goats, then dogs, then gave up on dogs because there were too many. I made up a game where I had to see a red thing before we passed the next milestone, or something bad would happen, and then spent forty kilometres in mild terror because there were no red things in western Odisha in 1998.

My mother had a rule. Every time the bus stopped for more than five minutes, I was allowed off. "Go," she would say, "but stay where I can see you," and I would jump down the steps and run. Not anywhere. Just run. Up and down beside the bus, around the tea stall, to the banyan tree and back. People watched me, amused. A boy running in circles at a bus stop in the heat, for no reason. One old man at a dhaba near Angul said to my father, "Your son has a horse inside him."

My father said, "Yes." Just that.

I liked that. I liked the idea of having a horse inside me. It seemed to explain a great deal.

* * *

Mamu's village was past Sambalpur, off the main road, along a track that the bus did not go on, so the last six kilometres were by bullock cart if Mamu had arranged one, or by a jeep that took eleven people and their luggage if he hadn't. The jeep was better. The jeep had no doors and the driver drove it like he was trying to escape from it.

And then there it was. A cluster of houses with tiled roofs around a pond, a temple to the goddess with a red flag on a bamboo pole, mango trees, tamarind trees, a huge peepal at the edge of the village where the old men sat on a cement platform all day doing nothing that I could see. Mamu's house was mud-walled and long, with a verandah all the way along the front and a courtyard behind with a tulsi plant in the middle on a little altar, and a well, and a cowshed, and a kitchen with a mud stove where my Maiji, Mamu's wife, cooked on wood and complained about it and would not have used gas if you had given it to her for free.

My cousins came out of the house like bees from a hive. There were seven of them. Or nine, depending on the year and how you counted, because some were Mamu's children and some were Mausi's, who lived in the next village and sent hers over for the summer, and some were from somewhere else entirely and nobody was quite sure how they were related, and nobody asked. They grabbed us. They grabbed the bags. They grabbed Chiku and carried her off somewhere and we didn't see her again for two days, except at meals.

"Bhubaneswar babu!" they called me, the city boy. I loved it. In Bhubaneswar I was the quiet one, the dreamer, the boy at the back of the assembly. In the village I was a celebrity. I had been to the Oberoi, or at least past it. I had a television that was colour. I had a Center Fresh card of Sachin, which I brought every year in its Gold Flake tin, and which my cousin Bulu held in both hands as if it were a relic from the temple.

* * *

In the village, nobody told me to sit still.

That was the whole thing. That was what I loved. We woke at five when the roosters started and the light was still grey, and went to the pond to bathe and swim, and the water was cool and green and full of things that touched your legs, and we screamed and splashed until Maiji shouted at us from the bank. We climbed the mango trees and ate raw green mangoes with salt and chilli powder that Bulu carried in a twist of paper in his pocket, and the sourness went up into your skull and made your eyes squeeze shut. We played cricket in a dry paddy field with a bat made from a coconut branch and stumps made from sticks, and here, too, I bowled, and here, too, my cousins thought I was mad. "You could bat, Bhubaneswar babu!" "I like bowling." "Arre, he likes running."

We went out with the cattle in the afternoon heat, to the edge of the forest, where the cowherd boys let us ride on the backs of the buffaloes. We stole jamun from someone's tree and our tongues went purple for a day. We lay in the shade of the peepal and listened to the old men talk about the price of paddy and the district collector and a tiger that had been seen in 1971 and had grown larger with every summer since.

In the evenings, there was no electricity, or very little, and the whole village sat out on verandahs and charpais under a sky so full of stars that it looked like spilled salt. Maiji told ghost stories. Mamu smoked a bidi and corrected her. My mother, who in Bhubaneswar was quiet and careful and always aware of the third floor listening, laughed out loud in the village. She laughed until she cried. She spoke in Sambalpuri, which she never spoke in the colony, a softer and more rolling language, full of words I half knew. "Kenta achis?" How are you? "Bhal achhe." I'm well. She was a different woman there. I think, looking back, she was the original one.

* * *

That was the summer, 1998, when I watered something for the first time on purpose.

There was a small guava sapling near the well in Mamu's courtyard that someone had planted in March and then forgotten about. By the time we arrived in May it was a thin stick with five or six leaves, the leaves curling at the edges and going yellow. Nobody was watering it. In the village, you did not water things in May. There was not enough water. The well was low, and every bucket went to the house and the cattle and the tulsi plant, which was a goddess and therefore came first.

"It's going to die," I said to Bulu.

"So?"

"So someone should water it."

"In this heat? It will die anyway. Wait for the rain. Rain will come next month and everything will grow." He was eleven, a year older than me, and very sure of himself.

I didn't say anything. I remembered the mango tree in the Dalda tin. I remembered Mrs. Das and her chalk.

The next morning I got up before the others and drew half a bucket from the well. The rope burned my hands. The bucket was heavy and swung, and I spilled half of what I had drawn on my feet, and then I carried what was left across the courtyard and poured it very slowly around the base of the guava sapling, watching the dry earth take it in and darken.

I did it every morning for three weeks. Bulu laughed at me, then got bored of laughing. Maiji saw me on the third day and said nothing, just looked, and on the fifth day she left a small brass pot by the well that was easier to carry. My mother saw me and said, "Why are you doing this?" and I said, "Because otherwise it will die before the rain comes," and she looked at me for a long moment and said, "Hm," which in my mother's language could mean a hundred things.

By the time we left, the leaves were green. Not many. Seven, maybe eight. But green, and flat, and new ones coming at the tip.

On the last morning, standing by the jeep with the luggage piled on the roof, I asked Bulu to keep watering it until the rain came.

"Why?"

"Just do it."

"What will you give me?"

I gave him my Robin Singh card. It was a good card. He looked at it, and at me, and back at the card, and said, "Okay," with the expression of someone who has got the better end of a deal with an idiot.

I don't know if he did it. I asked him in a postcard, and he wrote back about other things, about a match and a new calf and a wedding. But the next summer, when we went back, the guava tree was up to my shoulder. It had fruit on it the year after. By the time I was in college, it was taller than the cowshed.

Maiji used to send us a bag of guavas from it every winter, by whoever was travelling to Bhubaneswar. "From Max's tree," she would say on the phone, years later, when there were phones. "Max's tree is giving very sweet ones this year."

I never told her about Mrs. Das. She would not have understood why it mattered so much. Or she would have, completely, and I would not have been able to stand it.

* * *

We went to Puri, too, some years, but only for a day or two, and it was a different kind of trip. Sixty kilometres by bus, two hours, the road lined with coconut palms. The sea, which I saw for the first time at six and which frightened me so badly that I would not go nearer than ten feet from where the foam stopped. The temple of Jagannath, with its crowds and its smells and the pandas grabbing my father's arm. Khaja from a shop on Grand Road that came in a cardboard box tied with string and was gone before we got back to Bhubaneswar. Puri was a holiday. It was nice. But it was the kind of nice where you have to keep your shoes on and stay where your mother can see you.

The village was where the horse got to run.

On the bus home every year, nine hours east, back towards the heat and the four floors and the third bench from the window, I sat with my forehead against the glass and watched the hills shrink and go flat and turn back into paddy and then into half-built houses. And somewhere around Dhenkanal, every year, I felt it happen. A kind of folding. Something in me that had been opened out for three weeks folding itself back up to fit.

I never told anyone about the folding. I didn't have a word for it. I only knew that the closer we got to Bhubaneswar, the smaller I had to be.

Chapter 6

Outside the Oberoi

The rumour reached Survey of India Colony on a Thursday, which was the day the milkman came late, and so it is possible the milkman brought it.

"The Indian team is coming," Tukuna announced at the bottom of the Block C stairwell, out of breath, with the authority of someone who had run the whole way from Block A to say it. "To Bhubaneswar. They are staying at the Oberoi."

"Which team?"

"The Indian team, re! Cricket! Azhar, Sachin, everybody!"

There was a silence. The Rao brothers looked at each other. Rohan, who was sitting on the wall peeling the skin off a groundnut, stopped peeling.

"Who told you?"

"My uncle's friend drives a taxi from the airport."

This was considered unimpeachable. Taxi drivers knew everything. They were the cable television of the pre-cable world.

* * *

I have tried, since, to find out exactly what the team was doing in Bhubaneswar that year, and I cannot. I have looked in old newspapers and on the internet. I asked a cricket statistician once, a friend of a friend, who sent me a long email about fixtures in 1999 and concluded, very politely, that he couldn't find it either. Maybe it was a few of the players, not the whole team. Maybe it was a benefit match, or a promotional event, or a stopover. Maybe it was a different year. Maybe the taxi driver was wrong about some of it and the colony filled in the rest.

I don't care. I know what I saw. Or I know what I believe I saw, which, as I have learned in later life, is a slightly different thing and often more useful.

* * *

The Oberoi was in Jaydev Vihar, about a kilometre from the colony, along a road that in those days was straight and empty and lined with trees, with almost nothing on either side. It was the only real luxury hotel in Bhubaneswar, a long low building in the old Odisha temple style, with carved stone and a sloping roof and gardens that you could see over the boundary wall, so green they looked painted. We went past it on the school bus. We went past it on the Narmada when my father took us to Saheed Nagar. We had never been inside. Nobody we knew had been inside, except Mr. Panda, who claimed to have attended a wedding reception there in 1993 and described the toilets in such detail that we knew he was not lying.

Across the road from the colony, the other way, workers were building something big that year. A foundation had been dug, an enormous pit, and then pillars had gone up, and every afternoon we could see tiny men on bamboo scaffolding, carrying bowls of cement on their heads, climbing up and down like ants. Someone said it was going to be another hotel. Someone said it was a government building. Someone said it was going to be a shopping complex with a lift. A lift! We stood at the colony gate and watched the scaffolding go up, the women in their saris carrying bricks in stacks of eight on their heads, and imagined lifts.

"When that is finished," Rohan said, "Bhubaneswar will be like Bombay."

"Bombay has the sea."

"So does Puri."

"Puri is not Bhubaneswar."

"It's close enough, re."

That was what the city felt like then. It felt like it was waiting. Like the whole place was a scaffold, with something enormous about to be revealed, and nobody quite knew what it would be.

* * *

We went on the Saturday. Rohan, me, the two Raos and Tukuna. Five boys, aged between ten and twelve, with no permission from anyone. We told our mothers we were going to play cricket in the school ground, which was technically on the way.

We walked. Nobody had a cycle except the elder Rao, and he wasn't allowed to take it out of the colony. So we walked down the long straight road to Jaydev Vihar in the late morning, in the heat, in our chappals, with the trees on either side and the road shimmering ahead of us like water. Rohan had brought his bat, for reasons he couldn't explain. "In case Sachin wants to sign it." It was a cheap bat from the sports shop at Unit 2 market, with a rubber grip that had come half off, and the idea of Sachin Tendulkar signing it was so absurd and so wonderful that none of us laughed.

I had brought my Gold Flake tin. Inside it, at the top, was the Center Fresh card of Sachin. I had also brought, in my shirt pocket, the Wasim Akram cutout from the corner of my wall, though I had no idea why. Wasim was not coming. Wasim was in Pakistan. But I felt I ought to have him there.

When we were still a few hundred metres away, we heard the crowd.

* * *

I had never seen so many people in one place in Bhubaneswar outside of Rath Yatra. They filled the road in front of the hotel gate, spilled across onto the opposite side, climbed the trees, stood on the roofs of autorickshaws, sat on the boundary wall until the security men pulled them off and they climbed back on further down. Boys, mostly. Young men. A few fathers with small children on their shoulders. Police, in khaki, with lathis, looking bored and anxious at the same time. A man selling peanuts in paper cones, who must have had the best day of his life. Somebody had a transistor playing a commentary from some other match entirely, and nobody cared.

And everyone was looking at the same place. The hotel driveway, beyond the gate, which curved away between the gardens towards the entrance, which we couldn't see.

We pushed in. That's the only word for it. Five small boys, pushing in. I was the quickest and the thinnest, and I went first, and I found gaps between legs and under elbows that nobody else could see, and the others followed me in a chain, holding the backs of each other's shirts. The heat in the middle of the crowd was ferocious. The smell was sweat and hair oil and peanuts. Somebody's elbow hit me in the ear. Somebody stepped on Tukuna and he yelped and then pretended he hadn't.

After maybe twenty minutes, we were near the front. Not at the front. There were three or four rows of bigger boys in front of us, and in front of them a rope, and in front of the rope a line of police. But near.

"Can you see?" Rohan shouted in my ear.

"No."

"Get on my shoulders."

"You get on mine."

"You're lighter."

He was right. He crouched, and I climbed on, my knees on his shoulders and my hands on his head, and he stood up with a grunt, wobbling, the bat still in one hand, and suddenly I was above the crowd.

* * *

I could see the driveway. I could see the gardens, the fountains, the long low building with its carved stone. I could see a white Ambassador car with a red light on top, parked to one side. I could see men in safari suits standing near the entrance with walkie-talkies. I could see the doorman in his turban.

I could not see a single cricketer.

We waited. Rohan's shoulders shook under me. The crowd surged and settled, surged and settled, every time a car came down the driveway or a door opened or somebody shouted. It was always nothing. A waiter. A man with a file. A woman in a sari who was probably somebody's wife and who looked at the crowd with an expression of polite terror.

"Anything?" Rohan shouted.

"No."

"My neck is breaking, re."

"One more minute."

And then a door opened at the end of the building, a side door, not the main entrance, and three or four men came out together, walking fast, heads down, towards a bus I hadn't noticed parked in the shade.

The crowd made a sound. It wasn't a cheer. It was something lower, rising, like water starting to boil.

I saw a man in a blue tracksuit, short, with dark curly hair. I saw him for perhaps three seconds. He was walking quickly and talking to the man beside him, and he did not look at the crowd at all, and then just before he went up the steps of the bus he did. He turned his head and looked, for a moment, directly at the wall of us. And he lifted one hand. Not a wave, exactly. Just a hand, open, held up, the way you might acknowledge someone across a road.

Then he was inside the bus, and the door shut, and the crowd went mad.

"SACHIN!" Everyone was screaming it. "SACHIN! SACHIN!" The rope went down. The police shouted and swung their lathis at nothing. The crowd pushed forward, and Rohan staggered, and I grabbed his hair to stay on, and he shrieked, and we both went over sideways into the elder Rao brother, and all three of us ended up on the road in a pile with legs going past our faces.

Somebody stepped on my hand. I didn't feel it until later.

* * *

"Did you see him?" Rohan said, much later, when the bus had gone and the crowd had thinned and we were sitting on the kerb under a tree, all five of us, filthy and bruised and euphoric. "Did you actually see him? Tell the truth."

"I saw him."

"What did he look like?"

I thought about it. I wanted to tell it right. "Short."

"Everybody knows he's short."

"He looked at us."

"He looked at us?"

"He put his hand up. Like this." I showed him.

Rohan looked at my hand, open, held up. He was silent for a moment. He had seen nothing. He had been underneath me the whole time, with his face in the back of a stranger's shirt, holding me up.

"You're sure it was him?"

"I'm sure," I said, and I was. I was absolutely sure, and I remain sure, against every piece of evidence the statistician sent me, because some things you just decide to keep.

Then I said, "Sorry you didn't see."

He shrugged. "Next time you hold me up."

"Okay."

"Promise."

"Promise."

He picked up his bat. Nobody had signed it. There was a footprint on the blade, a big one, from a sandal, perfectly clear in the dust. He looked at it for a long time, and then he said, "Maybe this is his footprint," and we all laughed until Tukuna got the hiccups.

Rohan never cleaned that bat. He kept it until it fell apart, years later, and even then he kept the blade.

* * *

We walked home in the late afternoon heat, down the long straight road, past the scaffolding where the ant-men were climbing down for the day. My hand had started to hurt. Two of my knuckles were swollen and blue. I held it against my chest and didn't say anything.

At the colony gate, Mrs. Panda was waiting on her balcony. I don't know how she knew. She always knew.

"School ground, hah?" she called down. "Very nice cricket you played. Your mother is looking for you."

My mother was not looking for me. My mother was sitting on our balcony four floors up with Chiku in her lap, and she had obviously been sitting there for some time, and when I came up the stairs and in through the door with my hand against my chest and my shirt torn at the shoulder, she did not shout. She looked at my hand. She looked at my face. She said, "Show me."

I showed her. She turned my hand over gently, pressing the knuckles one by one, watching my face to see when I winced.

"Not broken," she said finally. "Go and wash. I'll put haldi."

I went to wash. When I came back she had the turmeric paste ready in a small steel bowl, warm, and she put it on my knuckles with two fingers and wrapped them in a strip of old sari.

"Did you see him?" she said, without looking up.

I stared at her.

"Mrs. Panda's nephew was there. He came back an hour ago. The whole colony knows."

"I saw him," I said.

She tied the knot. "Good," she said. "At least it was worth it."

* * *

My father, when he came home and was told, said nothing at dinner. He ate his rice and dalma and fish curry in his usual order, from the left side of the plate to the right, and he wiped his fingers and drank his water. Then, as he stood to go and wash his hands, he paused beside me.

"Which hand did he lift?"

"His right."

He nodded. "Right-handed," he said, and went to the sink.

Later that night, I took the Gold Flake tin out from under my slab and opened it and looked at the Center Fresh card of Sachin, the stiff over-coloured photo, the statistics on the back that I knew by heart. Then I opened my diary and wrote, under the light from the stairwell:

Saw Sachin. 3 seconds. He looked. Rohan held me up the whole time and saw nothing. Next time I hold him.

I looked at that last line for a long while. Then I took out the Wasim cutout from my shirt pocket, flattened it, and stuck it into the diary, on the same page, with a little rice paste from the kitchen. It is still there. It has gone brown and soft, and Wasim's face has worn away almost entirely, so that only his arm is left, coming over, about to let go of the ball.

Chapter 7

Two Days in October

There had already been one storm that month. People forget that.

In the middle of October 1999 a cyclone had come in further south, near Gopalpur, and Bhubaneswar had caught the edge of it: a day of wind and rain, some trees down, the power gone for a night. It was bad enough that school closed, which made it a good storm in my opinion. When it was over, the adults in the colony stood at the bottom of the stairwells and said what adults always said. "We were lucky." "It turned." "It was nothing." Mr. Panda told everyone that Bhubaneswar was protected by Lingaraj and would never be hit directly, and nobody argued with him, because it was a comforting idea and because he was in Accounts.

Ten days later, on the twenty-eighth, the radio started saying "super cyclone."

None of us had heard the word "super" used about weather before. Super was for Superman, for Super Commando Dhruv in the comics, for the Super Market that had opened near Unit 3 with a sliding glass door. It did not sound like something to be afraid of. It sounded like an exaggeration.

My father was afraid of it. I could tell because he went out on the Narmada on the evening of the twenty-eighth and came back with kerosene, two big cans of it, and candles, and a sack of rice, and a bag of chuda and gur, and three packets of Parle-G, and a box of matches for every room. He carried it all up the four floors in two trips, and on the second trip he stopped on the third-floor landing for a long time to catch his breath, and I watched him from the top of the stairs and pretended I wasn't watching.

"Bapa, is it going to come here?"

"It will come somewhere."

"Here?"

"Fill the buckets," he said. "All of them. And the drums. And the big handi."

* * *

It started on the morning of the twenty-ninth. A Friday. I remember it was a Friday because there would have been school, and there wasn't.

At first it was only wind. A strong wind, warm and wet, coming from the sea side, from the east, and pushing the clouds so low and fast over the colony that it looked as if the sky were being dragged somewhere against its will. The trees in the forest beyond the wall bent and straightened, bent and straightened. The clothes lines on the balconies snapped and sang. My mother went from balcony to balcony in the flat taking things in, the clothes, the pots, the Dalda tins with the tulsi and the chilli plant, while the wind tried to take them first.

Then the rain came, sideways.

I had seen a lot of rain. I had lain awake through four-day monsoon systems doing sums about the building drowning. This was not that. This was rain that did not fall. It travelled. It came across the gap between the buildings horizontally, in sheets, like something being thrown, and it hit the side of our block with a noise like gravel shovelled against a wall. It came in through the gaps around the balcony door. It came in through the ventilators above the windows, which had no glass, only a grille, and which nobody had ever thought of as a problem. Water ran down the inside of the walls. My mother and my sisters stuffed rags and old saris into every gap, and the rags turned dark and heavy and started to drip, and they wrung them into buckets and stuffed them back.

By the afternoon the power had gone. By evening the wind had changed its voice.

* * *

That is the thing I remember most. The sound.

People who were in it describe it in different ways. A train that never passes. A jet engine. Ten thousand people screaming. To me it sounded like the sky had found a single note and decided to hold it. It went on and on, a deep roaring, and then, every so often, it rose, it got higher and more furious, and the whole building seemed to brace itself, and you could hear things outside breaking, tearing, a crack like a gunshot and then a long groaning collapse, and then it would sink back to the note again. Hour after hour. All night.

We sat in the inner bedroom, the one with the smallest window, all six of us, on the floor, with one kerosene lantern turned low. My father had pushed the steel almirah across the bedroom window and wedged it there with the cot. My mother had Chiku in her lap, and Chiku was seven by then and too big for a lap and didn't care. Mamuni Nani, who was seventeen and in her last year of school, was holding Lipi Nani's hand, and Lipi Nani was holding mine. I don't think any of us decided to do that. Our hands just went there.

"What was that?" Lipi Nani said, every time there was a crash.

"A tree," said my father, every time.

Once he said, "A roof." He said it quietly, and nobody asked whose.

The building shook. I want to be careful here, because I was eleven and frightened, and it is possible that I felt the building shake when it was only me shaking. But I am fairly sure the building shook. The fourth floor was the top floor, there was nothing above us but the roof and the water tank, and the wind had the whole height of the block to lean on, and it leaned. The lantern swung on its hook. The water in the steel buckets in the corner moved in little rings.

I thought of the antenna on the roof. I thought of the water tank. I thought, very clearly, that the roof was going to come off, the whole slab, just lift up and go like the lid of a tiffin box, and then we would be sitting in the open in the dark in this.

"Bapa," I said. "Will the roof come off?"

He didn't answer straight away. That frightened me more than anything else that night. My father always answered straight away. If he didn't know, he said "I don't know," and he said it immediately, as if not knowing were a fact like any other.

Then he said, "This is a government building. It was built to a specification. I have seen the specification." He paused. "They used good steel in those days."

I have no idea if he had ever seen the specification. I have no idea if they used good steel. But my mother laughed, a short frightened laugh in the dark, and Lipi Nani laughed, and the roof stayed on.

* * *

At some point, very late, I couldn't bear sitting any more.

My legs had been bad for hours. Not just restless. Frantic. They were full of the noise, I think, full of the fear, and they had nowhere to go. I had been pressing my feet against the floor and my knees together, and counting, and holding Lipi Nani's hand so hard that she eventually said, "Ow, Max, ow," and pulled it away.

I got up.

"Sit down," said my mother.

"I need to go to the bathroom."

"Take the torch. Come straight back."

I took the torch. I didn't go to the bathroom. I went to the front room, the one with the balcony door, and I stood in the middle of it in the dark with the torch switched off, and I listened.

The balcony door was bolted top and bottom and had a chair jammed against it, and the rags along the bottom were soaked, and water was coming in under them in a thin steady stream that ran across the floor and around my feet. The door was rattling in its frame. Not a little. It was banging, again and again, as if someone were outside trying to get in. Through the gap at the top, where the frame had warped years ago, I could see a sliver of the outside. It was not dark. That surprised me. The sky had a kind of light in it, a dirty grey-orange, and against that light I could see shapes moving, flying past, things I couldn't identify. Leaves. Branches. A sheet of tin, spinning end over end, that went across the gap between the buildings like a playing card and was gone.

I stood there and watched it. I wasn't brave. I was so frightened I couldn't feel my hands. But standing up, standing there in the water, I could breathe again. My legs were quiet. I don't know how to explain it except that my body had decided that if it couldn't run, it would at least stand where it could see the thing it was afraid of.

My father came up behind me in the dark. I didn't hear him over the noise. I just felt his hand on my shoulder, heavy and warm.

He didn't tell me to come back. He stood with me, and we watched the gap at the top of the door together, the grey-orange light and the things flying through it.

After a long time he leaned down and said, close to my ear, so I could hear: "It always stops."

"I know."

"The question is what we do after."

"I know, Bapa."

He squeezed my shoulder, once, and went back to the others. I stayed a little longer. Then I went back too, and sat down next to Lipi Nani, and she took my hand again without saying anything, and I held it more gently this time.

* * *

It did not stop that night. It went on through the next day, Saturday the thirtieth, a little weaker but not much, the wind dropping and rising, the rain coming and going in great dark walls. We had chuda and gur for breakfast and lunch and dinner, soaked in water from the buckets. We used the bathroom by torchlight with a bucket of the stored water for flushing, and my father measured how much was left with his steel scale, dipping it into the drums and reading the wet line like a gauge, and said, "Two more days. If we are careful." He wrote it down. He wrote everything down that weekend, in a small notebook he kept in his shirt pocket. Water. Kerosene. Rice. The times the wind seemed to get stronger. I didn't know then that he was doing the same thing I did in my diary. I only knew that he looked calmer when he was writing.

We had no radio. The transistor's batteries had died on the first night, and nobody had thought to buy more. So we didn't know what was happening anywhere else. We didn't know that the storm had come ashore near Paradip, on the coast, about a hundred kilometres east of us, with winds of more than two hundred and fifty kilometres an hour. We didn't know about the surge, the wall of seawater that had gone miles inland across Jagatsinghpur and Ersama and the villages there, and taken everything. We didn't know that Bhubaneswar had been cut off from the world, its phones dead, its roads blocked, its power lines on the ground in every street. We didn't know that it would become one of the deadliest storms ever recorded in India, that thousands of people, around ten thousand, were dying or already dead within a few hours' drive of where we sat eating chuda by lantern light.

We only knew the noise. And then, at some point on the evening of the thirtieth, we knew that it was quieter.

Not quiet. Just quieter. The note had dropped. The crashes had stopped. The door had stopped banging and was only shivering in its frame. The rain was still falling, but falling now, downwards, the way rain is supposed to.

Nobody said anything for a while. We just sat and listened to it get quieter.

Then Chiku, who had been asleep in my mother's lap for hours, sat up, rubbed her eyes, and said in a perfectly ordinary voice: "Is it finished? Can I go to the balcony?"

My mother started to cry. I had never seen her cry before. Not once. She cried with no sound at all, just tears, her face completely still, and she held Chiku and rocked, and my father put his hand on the back of her neck and left it there.

"Not yet," he said to Chiku. "In the morning."

* * *

In the morning, we went out onto the balcony.

I will try to describe it, though I have tried before and never got it right.

The forest was gone. Not all of it. But the forest as I knew it, the wall of green beyond the colony boundary that had been there my whole life, the place of jackals and vultures and the low eyes in the monsoon dusk, was a field of broken wood. The trees were down. Hundreds of them. They lay in every direction, crossed over each other like spilled matchsticks, their roots in the air with the earth still clinging to them. Some were snapped halfway up, the trunks splintered white and raw. The ones still standing had no leaves. Not one. They stood bare and grey against the sky like it was the middle of a winter we had never had.

The colony road was a river again, but a river full of things. Branches. Tin sheets. A door. Somebody's Dalda tin, with a chilli plant still in it. An electric pole lay across the road at an angle, its wires trailing in the water. Two of the big trees by the colony gate were down, one of them on top of the gate itself, crushing it flat. The roof of the colony office had gone, and the black telephone with the lock on its dial was somewhere in the wreckage, and Behera the clerk, I learned later, stood in the ruins on the Monday morning and wept.

The scaffolding across the road, where the ant-men had been building their maybe-hotel, was gone. Just gone. Not fallen. Gone. Only the concrete pillars were left, grey and bare.

On the balcony of the next block, Mrs. Rao was standing in her nightie with her hands over her mouth. Below us, on the third floor, I could hear Mrs. Panda crying and Mr. Panda saying, "It's all right, it's all right, it's all right," over and over, as if he were trying to convince the building.

I stood at the grille with my family and looked at it. Nobody spoke.

The sky over the forest, over what had been the forest, was clean. Washed. The palest blue. It was the kind of sky you only get after something enormous has passed, and it was beautiful, and I hated it for being beautiful.

Then my father said, "Max. Get the buckets."

"What?"

"The buckets. All of them. The tank on the roof has probably gone. There will be no water. We need to find water." He was already putting on his chappals. "Come."

Chapter 8

Water

There is a tube well at the bottom of the hill behind Block F, near the edge of the RRL boundary. I say "is" because I went back to look for it twenty years later and found it still there, painted green now, with a little cement apron around it and a sign with a politician's name. In November 1999 it was the most important object in Survey of India Colony.

The overhead tanks on every block had either been damaged or emptied, and without power there was no pump to fill them again. The municipal supply had stopped. The ponds and the drains were full, but full of a brown water with things floating in it that nobody wanted to think about. Somebody said the tube well behind Block F still worked, and by the time my father and I got there with our buckets on that first morning, there were already thirty people in a line.

It was a hand pump. You worked a long iron handle up and down, and after eight or nine strokes the water came, cold and clear and smelling faintly of iron, in a gush that filled half a bucket and then slowed to a trickle so that you had to pump again. One person pumped, another held the bucket. The line moved slowly. People talked in low voices about what they had heard. Cuttack was cut off. Puri was cut off. The airport was closed. There were no trains. On the coast, something terrible had happened, though nobody knew what, and the rumours were so big and so bad that people repeated them and then shook their heads as if to say they didn't believe them, and then repeated them again.

When our turn came, my father pumped and I held. Then I pumped and he held. Four buckets, two each.

Then we had to carry them up.

* * *

Four floors. I had run those stairs ten thousand times. I had never carried anything heavier than a cloth bag of Pepsi bottles up them. A full steel bucket of water is about fifteen kilos. I was eleven, wiry, the boy aunties called lean-but-he-eats. On the first flight I thought it was fine. On the second my arms began to shake. On the third the handle was cutting into my fingers, and the water was slopping over the rim onto my feet and the steps, and my shoulders were on fire. On the fourth I had to stop at every landing and put the bucket down and stand with my hands on my knees.

My father was behind me with his two. He was forty-six that year and had a bad knee, and he was breathing in a way I had never heard before, a deep scrape at the bottom of every breath. He did not stop at the landings. He kept going, slowly, one step at a time, his face completely set.

We got to the top. My mother took the buckets at the door and poured them into the drums. We stood in the doorway, both of us dripping, both of us blowing like buffaloes.

"How many more?" I said.

My father looked at the drums. He looked at his notebook. "For six people. Drinking, cooking, washing, bathroom." He did the sum. "Twelve buckets a day. If we are careful."

Twelve buckets a day. Four floors. Every day until the power came back, and nobody knew when that would be.

I looked at my father's knee.

"I'll do it," I said.

"We'll both do it."

"No. I'll do most of it. You do the drinking water." I didn't know where this came from. "You do the pumping. I'll carry."

He looked at me for a long moment. I think he wanted to argue. I think he was too tired to.

"Six," he said finally. "You do six. I do six."

"Eight and four."

"Seven and five."

"Okay." And I took the empty buckets from my mother and went back down the stairs before anyone could change their mind.

* * *

The power was out for a long time. I have seen different figures since, and the truth is it varied by area; parts of the city were back within a week or two, others waited much longer. In our block it felt like forever. Every evening we sat by lantern light. Every morning we went down to the tube well before dawn to beat the line.

I carried water every day. I want to tell this honestly, which means saying that I did not do it gladly every day. Some days I hated it. Some mornings my arms still hurt from the day before, and my fingers had blisters across the middle joint that burst and wept and had to be wrapped in strips of cloth, and I lay on the floor at five in the morning when my father touched my shoulder and thought, very clearly, why me. Why should I do this. Mamuni Nani and Lipi Nani are older. Why is it the boy. Why is it always the boy who takes the heavy job.

And then I got up and did it. Every time. Because I knew the answer, even then, even before I could have said it. It was the same answer as the bowling. Nobody was going to make me stronger. Nobody was going to hand me a body that could carry things, or a self that could get up at five in the morning when it hurt. The only way was through. You took the heavy end. You did it until it stopped being heavy, and then you noticed that it had stopped being heavy, and then you took something heavier.

Some of the other boys in the colony carried water. Most of them did it with a lot of complaint, and some of them did it with none, and Rohan did it for exactly two days and then developed a mysterious back pain that kept him in bed until the power came back. I didn't blame him. I teased him about it for twenty years, but I didn't blame him.

My sisters carried too, it should be said. Smaller buckets, handis, the brass kalsi on the hip the way my mother had learned in the village. Chiku carried a little plastic jug, full to the brim, up four floors with her tongue between her teeth, concentrating so hard that she once walked straight past our door and up to the roof. It was all of us, in the end. But I took the most. I made sure of that.

* * *

Something happened to the colony in those weeks.

It is hard to describe. The same people who had spent years watching each other through half-open doors, counting each other's purchases, gossiping about each other's children's marks, stood in line together at the tube well in the dark and passed buckets. Mrs. Panda, who had never once said a kind word to my mother that didn't have a hook in it, came up the stairs on the third day with a pot of khichdi made on a kerosene stove and stood in our doorway and said, "Your son has carried water for my husband twice. I saw him." And put the pot down and left before my mother could answer.

I had carried water for Mr. Panda twice. I hadn't thought anyone noticed. His knee was worse than my father's and he was too proud to say so, and I had seen him on the second-floor landing with a bucket, not moving, and taken it from him without saying anything. Mrs. Panda had seen it from somewhere. Of course she had. She always knew.

Mr. Rao, who was an engineer in some department, rigged a hand-cranked dynamo from somebody's cycle to charge a car battery, so that the block could run one bulb in the stairwell at night. The elder Rao brother and I pedalled it for an hour each evening, in turns, and for that hour the stairwell glowed a weak orange and people sat on the steps under it just to be in the light.

The men of the colony went out with axes and saws in the second week to clear the road. My father went with them. He came back each evening with sawdust in his hair and his hands torn, and said nothing about it, and my mother put Boroline on his palms after dinner without being asked.

Nobody knew anything from the outside. There were no newspapers for days. When they came, they came in a bundle, a week of them at once, and my father read them by lantern light in a silence that made the rest of us go quiet too. He read about Ersama. About the villages. About the surge and the bodies and the children. He read some of it aloud and then stopped reading aloud. He folded the paper very carefully, the way he folded maps, and sat for a while looking at the lantern.

"We were lucky," he said.

Nobody argued with him this time.

* * *

The first time I went to school after the cyclone, it was a different school.

KV had been closed for weeks. When it reopened the ground was still full of fallen branches piled in heaps for collection, and the boundary wall had come down in two places, and the roof of the chemistry lab had a hole in it the size of a car. Half the trees along the drive had gone. The neem outside my classroom window, the one I had spent years looking at instead of the blackboard, had lost a whole limb, the big one on the left side, and stood lopsided, a raw yellow wound where the branch had been.

But it was standing.

At lunch I went to the boundary wall, the one where the school had planted saplings in the monsoon two years before. Section B's neem, the one the peon had grumbled over all through that first May. I hadn't thought about it in months. I half expected it to be gone.

It was there. It was about as tall as me now. It had lost most of its leaves and a few of its top shoots, and one side had been flattened by a fallen branch, but the trunk was straight and the roots were in the ground. Along the wall, the other saplings, the ones that had been planted in the same rain and then left to God, were mostly gone. Snapped, or pulled out, or flattened. A few twigs. A gap.

The peon was there, by chance, sitting on the broken wall eating his lunch out of a steel box. He saw me looking.

"That one," he said, pointing his chin at the neem. "Strong one."

"Why did it survive?"

He shrugged. "Roots," he said. "I had to water that one every day in the summer, the first year. Every day, in the heat, because it was looking like it would die. So the roots went down looking for the water." He made a gesture with his hand, fingers pointing down, going deep. "The ones that only got rain, their roots stayed on top. Lazy roots. Wind comes, pulls them out like hair."

He went back to his lunch.

I stood there for a long time. Then I went back to class, and sat on my bench, and looked out at the lopsided neem through the window, and didn't hear a word of the lesson. Mrs. Das was not my teacher any more. She had been transferred, somebody said, to Rourkela or Jharsuguda. I was glad. I don't think I could have sat in her class that afternoon without standing up and saying something I would have been punished for.

That night I wrote in my diary, by the lantern:

School neem survived. Peon says because it was watered in summer. Roots go down looking. Lazy roots get pulled out.

And then, underneath, after a long time, pressing so hard that the pen nearly went through the page:

I was right.

I didn't write anything else for a week. I didn't need to.

* * *

The power came back to our block on a Tuesday evening, without warning. Every light in the flat came on at once, every switch we had left on and forgotten, and the fan began to turn, slowly at first and then faster, and the BPL television in the front room blinked on to a snowy, roaring screen. The whole colony cheered. You could hear it from every block, every balcony, a sound I have never heard before or since, hundreds of people cheering for a light bulb.

Chiku ran from room to room switching things on and off and on again, shrieking with joy. My mother sat down on the floor of the kitchen and laughed. My father stood under the fan in the front room with his face turned up, his eyes closed, the air moving his thin hair.

And I went to the balcony and looked out at the colony. Lights in every window. Lights in the stairwells. Beyond the boundary wall, where the forest had been, there was only darkness and the shapes of the broken trees.

Tomorrow, I thought, I won't have to carry water.

And then, to my own surprise, I thought: I'll miss it.

I didn't understand that for years. I think I understand it now. For a few weeks after the cyclone, I had known exactly what I was for. The task had been simple, and heavy, and mine, and every day I could see the drums fill up and know that I had done it. Nobody had compared me to anyone. Nobody had asked my marks. The question had been only: can you carry it up four floors. And the answer had been yes.

It was the last time, for a long time, that the answer would be so simple.

Chapter 9

Marks

"Ninety-one," said Mrs. Panda. "Ninety-one percent. In the boards. Her photo is in the Samaja."

She was talking about her niece, who lived in Cuttack and whom none of us had ever met, but who had been held up to every child in Survey of India Colony for two years as an example of what was possible if you only applied yourself. My mother nodded and said, "Very good, very good," and went on cleaning rice on the steel thali in her lap, picking out the small stones with a fingernail. Mrs. Panda stood in the doorway a while longer, waiting for my mother to say something about my marks, and when she didn't, she said, "And Max? Half-yearly results came?"

"They came," said my mother.

"And?"

"He passed."

Mrs. Panda waited. My mother picked out another stone.

"Hm," said Mrs. Panda, and went downstairs.

* * *

I passed. That was the truth about me between the ages of twelve and seventeen, from the first year after the cyclone to the day I finished Class XII. I passed everything. I never failed a subject. I never came near the top either. My marks lived in the sixties and low seventies like a family in a two-room flat, comfortable enough, nothing to boast about, never likely to move.

And everybody around me, it seemed, was moving.

Rohan was the first to go. In Class VIII he suddenly got serious. I don't know what happened. Perhaps his father sat him down. Perhaps he simply grew up a year before I did. He stopped coming down for cricket on weekday evenings and started going to tuition at a place near Unit 4 that had a sign saying JEE / AIEEE / OJEE FOUNDATION COURSE and a man at the door who checked your fees receipt. His marks went from the seventies to the eighties to the nineties. In Class X he got ninety-two in the boards and his photo was in the Samaja, a small one, smiling with his mouth closed because he had braces.

"Saala, I didn't even know you could study," I told him.

"Neither did I," he said, and grinned, and the braces flashed.

Mamuni Nani finished her BSc and got a government job in the state secretariat on her first attempt at the exam, which in our family was received as if she had walked on the moon. Lipi Nani went to nursing college in Cuttack and wrote us long letters about the hostel food. Even Chiku, the baby, the one who had carried water up four flights with her tongue between her teeth, came home from her school with report cards that made my mother put them in the almirah, in the special drawer.

My report cards did not go in the special drawer. They went in the ordinary drawer, with the electricity bills.

* * *

It's important to say that nobody was cruel to me. My family loved me. That has to be clear, because the story I am about to tell could sound like a story about a boy whose family didn't, and it isn't. My mother made me luchi on Sundays and saved me the biggest piece of fish. My father took me to the Kalinga Stadium to watch a Ranji match and bought me a Gold Spot at the interval. My sisters fought with me and defended me in equal measure, and when a boy in Class IX called me "Fail-Pass Max" in front of Lipi Nani's friend, Lipi Nani found out his name and spoke to his elder sister and he never said it again.

They loved me. They just didn't know what to do with me. And they worried, all the time, quietly, in a way I could feel through the walls.

I could feel it at dinner when the conversation turned to someone else's son who had cleared an entrance exam. I could feel it when relatives visited and the first question, always, before "how are you" and sometimes before "hello," was "Which class? What percentage?" I could feel it in the particular quality of my father's silence when he signed my report card. He never shouted. He never said I was a disappointment. He read every mark, slowly, from top to bottom, as if checking a survey sheet for errors, and then he signed his name at the bottom in his careful hand with the seven that had a line through it, and handed it back to me, and said, "Next time."

That was all. "Next time." Two words. I would rather he had hit me.

* * *

The world changed around us in those years, faster than anything had changed before.

Cable came to our flat in 2001, finally, a wire through the balcony grille, and with it a hundred channels. Chiku discovered Cartoon Network and a show called Junior G that came on in the evenings, about a boy with powers, and watched it with the same holy silence we had given to Shaktimaan. In 2002 a new sports channel started showing WWE, and suddenly every boy in the colony knew the Rock's catchphrases and was doing them at each other on the stairs, and the old cards I had in the Gold Flake tin, the ones from the nineties, became antiques overnight. The construction site across the road, the one whose scaffolding the cyclone had taken, rose again, and got finished, and became an office building with glass windows that reflected the colony back at itself. The forest beyond the wall started to be cleared, slowly, the broken trees dragged away, the ground flattened, until there was a wide dusty space where the jackals had been, and people started calling it Janata Maidan and holding rallies there.

Mobile phones arrived. Not for us. For a few men in safari suits, at first, who walked around the Unit 9 market talking into big plastic bricks very loudly so that everybody would know. Then for doctors. Then for Rohan's father, who got one in 2003 and treated it like a small, beloved animal.

And the conversation changed. In the nineties, safe had meant a government job. Now, suddenly, safe meant engineering. Or medicine. Everyone was going to be an engineer. Odisha had started opening engineering colleges everywhere, in every town, colleges in buildings that had been warehouses a year before, and every family in the colony wanted their son in one. The question was no longer "What will you be?" It was "Engineering or medical?" As if there were nothing else. As if those two doors were the only doors in the whole building, and every other door was a wall.

* * *

I tried. I want that on the record too.

In Class XI I took science, PCM with biology, because that was what you did, and I went to tuition at the same place near Unit 4 that Rohan had gone to, where the man at the door checked my receipt. I sat in a room with sixty other students and a ceiling fan that didn't work, and a teacher who wrote integrals on a green board so fast that the chalk screamed, and I tried to follow.

I couldn't. Not because I couldn't understand. I would understand the first step, and the second, and then somewhere around the third my mind would slide sideways, like a cycle on wet mud. I would find myself looking at the window. At a crow on the wire outside. At the girl two rows ahead who had a habit of twisting a pencil into her hair. At the shape of a cloud. And then I would look back at the board and the teacher would be four steps further on, and the integral had become something else entirely, a long tangle of symbols that I could no longer find the beginning of.

And my legs. My legs were still there. Sixteen years old, nearly six feet tall now, and still they twitched and bounced under the desk, still they wanted to go. I ran in the mornings before tuition, around the colony and down the long road towards Jaydev Vihar, past the Oberoi gate where I had once sat on Rohan's shoulders, and back. I ran until my lungs burned. It helped for an hour. Then I sat down at the desk and the horse woke up again and kicked at the walls.

Everybody else could sit. That was the thing I could not get over. I would look around the tuition room, at the sixty heads bent over sixty notebooks, and every one of them seemed to be still, completely still, like water in a bucket. I was the only one rippling. I thought: they have something I don't. Some part of the brain, some wire, some switch. They were born with it, and I wasn't, and that is why they are moving forward and I am standing here.

I thought: I'm lazy.

I thought it so often that it stopped being a thought and became a fact, like my height or my blood group. Max is tall. Max is B-positive. Max is lazy. If I had only tried harder, I told myself, if I had only sat still, if I had only had the discipline Rohan had found in Class VIII, I would be moving too. The problem was not my brain. The problem was my character. I was the boy who wanted to bowl because he could not sit and bat.

That is a terrible thing to believe about yourself at sixteen. I believed it for about nine years.

* * *

I wrote in the diary less in those years. It was a different diary by then, the third or fourth; the thin one with the film actress on the cover had filled up long before, and I had moved through a Classmate notebook and a spiral-bound one with a broken spine, and they all lived together under my slab, behind the books, in a plastic bag that had once held a sari. I wrote less because I didn't like what I was writing. When I opened the diary, the pen went to the same places. Couldn't focus again. Tuition test, 11 out of 30. Rohan got 28. What is wrong with me.

One entry from that period says, in its entirety: Bapa said "next time." There is no next time. There is only this time, again.

I'm not proud of that one. But I don't tear it out.

* * *

The entrance exams came in the spring of 2005.

I won't go through them all. I took the state engineering exam, and the national one, and the medical one because my mother asked me to and I couldn't say no to her face. I sat in exam halls in Cuttack and in a school in Nayapalli whose name I've forgotten, with a clipboard and two HB pencils and an admit card that had my photo stapled to it, a boy who looked surprised. I filled in bubbles. I filled them in well, at first. Then, in every exam, somewhere around the forty-minute mark, I felt the slide begin. The paper went soft. The questions looked back at me like strangers. I looked up at the clock on the wall. I looked at the window. I looked down at the paper and found I'd lost my place.

The results came in May. The heat was terrible that year, nearly as bad as the year of the bus stopping and everybody sleeping underneath it. I found my results in a newspaper supplement at the shop on the corner of the main road, the same shop where I had bought Pepsi bottles in a cloth bag. I ran my finger down the list in the shade of the shop awning for a long time.

My rank in the engineering exam was so far down that the colleges I could get into were the ones in the warehouses. My rank in the medical exam was not a rank. It was a number with five digits.

Rohan's rank was eleven hundred and something. He got into a good engineering college in Rourkela, computer science, and his father distributed sweets to every flat in the colony in little cardboard boxes. Our box was rasgullas. I ate one, standing at the kitchen window, and it was very good, and I hated it.

* * *

That night my father and I sat on the balcony after everyone else had gone to bed. He had his glass of tea, though it was late. I had nothing. The air was hot and still, and the place where the forest had been was flat and dark, and on the other side of it the lights of the new office building glowed in a long row.

"What do you want to do?" he said.

It was the first time anyone had asked me that. Not "what will you do," not "engineering or medical." What do you want.

I didn't have an answer ready. I had never needed one. I sat there for a long time.

"I like living things," I said eventually. It came out sounding stupid. "I like how they grow. I like the trees. I like... I don't know. Biology was the only one where I wasn't always looking out the window."

He didn't say anything. He drank his tea.

"There is a course," I said. "Biotechnology. In a college here. BSc. It's new. Not many people do it."

"Biotechnology," he said, slowly, testing the word.

"It's science," I said quickly. "Real science. Genetics, microbes, plants. It's the future, some people say."

"Some people say," he repeated.

"I know it's not engineering."

"No," he said. "It is not engineering."

The silence went on so long that I thought he had fallen asleep. Then he set his glass down on the floor, very carefully, the way he did everything.

"Do it properly," he said. "Whatever it is. Do it properly."

And he went inside.

I stayed on the balcony until the sky started to go grey. I didn't cry. I sat with my back against the wall where I had written the first line in the first diary, with the stairwell light coming through the grille, and I tried to feel what I was supposed to feel, which was relief. He had said yes. He had not shouted. He had not said, as some fathers in the colony would have said, that I had wasted his money and shamed his name.

But I had heard what he hadn't said. I had heard the silence between "It is not engineering" and "Do it properly." It was the length of every rasgulla in every cardboard box in Survey of India Colony. It was the length of the whole long straight road to the Oberoi.

I heard that silence for years.

Chapter 10

Oh, That's Interesting

"Biotechnology," said my Bada Bapa, my father's elder brother, at a wedding in Bargarh in December 2005. "Oh. That's interesting." And he tilted his head, slightly, to one side, the way you might look at a painting you have been told is valuable.

I heard "Oh, that's interesting" perhaps two hundred times over the next three years. I started keeping count in the back of my diary, the way I had once kept count of the water on the steps of Block D. It was said by uncles and aunties and neighbours and my sisters' in-laws and once by a ticket collector on a train to Sambalpur who asked what I studied and seemed genuinely sorry for me. It always came with the tilt. I came to think of the tilt as the gesture of my generation's parents when confronted with anything that was not engineering or medicine: a slight angling of the head, as if the thing you had said had arrived at an unexpected slope.

Some of them followed up. "So what job will you get?"

"Research, maybe. Pharma. Agriculture."

"Hm. Government?"

"Maybe."

"Hm." The tilt deepened. "And Rohan, he's in Rourkela, no? Computer science?"

"Yes."

"Very good. Very good boy."

* * *

The college was on the outskirts of the city, past Patia, in a building that was still being finished when I joined. On my first day there was no glass in the windows of the second floor, and the corridors smelled of wet cement and paint, and a group of labourers was laying tiles in the principal's office while the principal stood in the doorway in a tie, giving a welcome speech to the new batch about the bright future of the biotechnology sector in India.

There were thirty-eight of us. Most were from Bhubaneswar or Cuttack, a few from Berhampur and Balasore, two from Bihar. Most, like me, had not got the rank they wanted. You could tell on the first day who knew it and who was pretending. The ones who knew it sat at the back. The ones who were pretending sat at the front and asked a lot of questions.

And then there were the ones who had chosen it. Four or five of them. They had got decent ranks in the entrance exams and turned down engineering colleges to come here, because they actually wanted to do biotechnology, and their parents had let them. They were the ones who already knew what a plasmid was. They sat in the second row and took notes in three colours. One of them, a girl from Cuttack called Sneha, had read a book by an American scientist about the double helix over the summer holidays, for fun, and talked about Rosalind Franklin as if she were a friend who had been treated badly.

I sat in the fourth row. Not the front, not the back. Exactly in the middle of the classroom, the way my marks had always sat in the middle of every list.

* * *

I loved some of it. That surprised me.

I loved the labs. The first time I put a drop of pond water on a slide and saw, under the microscope, a whole world of moving things, little transparent creatures spinning and sliding and bumping into each other, I forgot where I was. I forgot to write anything down. I sat at that microscope for so long that the lab assistant, an old man called Sahu who had worked in some government institute for thirty years before retiring to this, came and stood behind me and said, "What are you looking at, baba? There's nothing there."

"There's everything there," I said, without looking up.

Sahu was silent for a moment. Then he laughed, a dry pleased laugh, and patted my shoulder, and went away. After that he always let me stay late in the lab.

I loved the plant tissue culture, where you took a little piece of a leaf, a scrap the size of a fingernail, and put it in a jar of clear jelly with the right hormones, and waited, and after a few weeks it became a whole plant. A whole new plant from almost nothing, from a scrap. I watched those jars on the shelf in the culture room every day like a man watching a cricket score.

I loved the idea of it. Of all of it. That living things had a code, and that the code could be read, and that a boy from a fourth-floor quarter could learn to read it.

The problem was everything else.

* * *

The problem was the theory classes, the long hours in rooms with ceiling fans, where professors read from notes they had written decades earlier and copied onto overhead projector sheets that had gone yellow at the edges. Biochemistry. Biostatistics. Molecular biology with its endless diagrams of enzymes, each one a sort of tangled ribbon with arrows coming off it. I would sit down at nine-thirty in the morning with my notebook open and my pen ready and the absolute determination that this time I would follow it all, and by ten the slide would begin.

It was the same slide as in tuition. The same as in the exam halls. The words on the projector would start to drift away from each other. I thought about that a lot. I thought: he served something faithfully for thirty years and it ended with a shawl and a scrap dealer. And then I thought: and what am I going to give him? What is his son going to be?

I didn't know. I didn't know, I didn't know, I didn't know. I wrote it in the diary like that, three times in a row, and then I closed the diary and went out and walked.

Chapter 11

The Walks

From the rented flat in Nayapalli to the edge of the old colony was a little under four kilometres, if you went by the main road, and a little over five if you went the way I went, which was through the back lanes, past the Ram Mandir in Unit 9, past the gate of my old school, along the edge of Janata Maidan and down the road towards the 7th Battalion and the TV centre, and back.

I would finish dinner, and help my mother with the dishes, and sit on the small balcony for a few minutes looking at the wall of the building opposite, and then I would feel it come up through my legs, that old pressure, and I would say "I'm going for a walk," and my mother would say "Take the torch," and I would go.

She never asked where. I think she knew.

* * *

I walked fast at first. Then slower, once the noise in my head had settled into something I could hear over. It was always the same noise, more or less. A set of questions that went around and around like the fan in Quarter No. 8, at whatever speed the voltage allowed.

Will I get a job.

Will anyone give a job to a boy with sixty-two percent in biotechnology from a college with no glass in its second-floor windows.

Did I ruin it. Was the moment I said "biotechnology" on the balcony the moment I ruined it. Should I have taken the warehouse engineering college. Should I have taken a drop year and tried again, like Tukuna, who took two drop years and got into a dental college in Bhubaneswar and was now, according to his mother, a doctor.

What happens to Bapa's pension if something goes wrong. Who looks after Bou and Bapa when they are old, if Max has no job. Chiku is still in school. Lipi Nani is going to get married next year, and weddings cost money, and the boy's family is from Cuttack and has already mentioned, politely, twice, what their nephew's wedding cost.

Did I put the whole family in danger by being who I am.

That last one was the one I walked the longest for.

* * *

The city had changed so much by then that some evenings I barely recognised it. Janata Maidan was a real maidan now, a great flat field of dust and dry grass with lamp posts around it and a stage at one end for rallies. In December there was a trade fair there with a giant wheel, and the whole of Bhubaneswar went, and the smell of fried things and diesel generators drifted for kilometres. In the place where I had once watched four vultures take an afternoon over a dead cow, there was a stall selling plastic toys from China and another selling Kashmiri shawls.

Xerox and lamination. Hair salon (Gents). A dhaba with a hand-painted sign promising Chinese and Mughlai and South Indian, all three. But at night you could still hear something moving in there, a rustle, a branch, and I liked that. I liked that something wild had held on.

I would stand at the gate, the new one, the one they had put up after the cyclone crushed the old one, and look in at the blocks. They looked smaller than I remembered. Everything does, people say, but it's true. The road between Block C and Block D where we had played cricket was so narrow now in my eyes that I couldn't imagine bowling down it. The electric pole was still there. The line we had scratched on the tar was long gone, paved over twice. On the fourth floor of our old block, Quarter No. 8, there was a new family; I could see their clothes on the line on the balcony, a man's shirts and a woman's saris and a child's school uniform, a blue one, KV blue, hanging upside down by the hem.

I played cricket on Sundays in a ground in Patia with a group of boys from the hostel, and still bowled, and still got called mad for it. I laughed. * * *

One night in my third year, I walked further than usual. I don't know why. I passed the colony gate and kept going, along the road towards Jaydev Vihar, the long straight road that I had walked with Rohan and the Rao brothers and Tukuna in 1999 to see Sachin.

The Oberoi was still there, at the end, behind its wall and its gardens, but there was a big mall now opposite, with a multiplex and a McDonald's whose golden sign hung in the night like a second moon. It was the feeling that I had once been a person who would push through a crowd of a thousand people in the heat to see something for three seconds. Who would climb on his friend's shoulders, and grab his hair, and fall in the road and get his hand stepped on, and come home and say it was worth it. That person had existed. I had been him. I didn't know where he had gone.

I thought: he wasn't lazy. That boy. Whatever he was, he wasn't lazy.

It was the first time the thought had come. It didn't stay. It went away almost immediately, the way the first drops of a monsoon go away into hot earth without leaving a mark. I walked home and went to bed and woke up the next morning and was lazy again, and stupid again, and fourth from the bottom.

But it had come. I wrote it down, the next night, very small, in the corner of a page, almost as if I were hiding it from myself.

Outside the Oberoi. That boy was not lazy.

* * *

My father was waiting up when I got home that night. He was sitting on the small balcony in Nayapalli with the light off, in his vest and lungi, and he didn't say anything as I came in, and I nearly didn't see him.

"Bapa? Why are you awake?"

"It is late," he said.

"I was walking."

"I know." A pause. "Every night, you are walking."

I stood in the doorway to the balcony. I didn't know what to say.

"When I was your age," my father said, "I used to walk also. In the village. In the evenings. Along the canal, there is a road. Five kilometres to the next village and five back." He was looking at the wall of the building opposite, not at me. "My father used to say, that boy is walking to Cuttack and back every night, he will wear out his chappals and then what."

"What were you thinking about?"

He was quiet for a while.

"Whether I would get a job," he said. "Whether I would be able to look after my mother. Whether I was good enough." He moved his hand, a small gesture, the hand of a man who had spent thirty-four years checking numbers. "Same things."

The building opposite had a light on in one window, a kitchen, a woman washing something at a sink. Somewhere a dog was barking. It was the most my father had ever told me about himself, and I could feel how much it had cost him, and I didn't want to waste it by speaking.

After a long time, he said, "You will find it."

"Find what?"

"Whatever you are walking for." He got up, slowly, his bad knee clicking. He put his hand on my head for a second, the way he used to when I was small, and then took it away. "But you will not find it walking. At some point you will have to sit."

He went inside.

I stayed on the balcony until I heard him begin to snore. Then I took out my diary and wrote down what he had said, exactly, word for word.

At some point you will have to sit.

I didn't know yet that he was going to be right. I didn't know yet what would make me.

Chapter 12

The Sitting

The thing that made me sit was a failure. It usually is.

In the fifth semester, in December 2007, I failed Biostatistics. Not barely. Properly. Twenty-three out of a hundred, where the pass mark was forty. It was the first subject I had ever failed in my life. My name was on the noticeboard outside the department office with a small "R" next to it in red ink, which meant re-appear, which meant a supplementary exam in July, which meant that if I failed again I would not get my degree on time, and would have to do the semester again, and would graduate a year behind everyone, a year behind Sneha and the boys from the back and the girl with the laugh like a bicycle bell.

I stood in front of that noticeboard for a long time. Other students came and looked and went away. Some of them looked at me. Nobody said anything. The red "R" sat next to my name like a drop of blood.

I didn't go for a walk that night. That was the first sign that something had changed, though I didn't know it. I went home on the bus and walked up the two floors to the flat in Nayapalli and put my bag down, and my mother looked at my face and said, "Kana hela?" and I said, "I failed a subject," and she put down the vegetables she was cutting and came and stood in front of me and put both her hands on my face.

She didn't say anything. She just held my face for a second, and looked at me, and then she went back to the vegetables.

My father, when he came in from his evening walk to the market, was told by my mother in the kitchen in a low voice. I heard him say, "Hm." Then he came out and sat on his chair on the balcony and opened the Samaja and read it, and didn't say anything to me all evening, and at dinner he ate from left to right and drank his water and washed his hands, and as he passed me on his way to bed, he stopped.

"July?" he said.

"July."

He nodded. And then he said, not "next time," but something else.

"Then you have six months."

* * *

The textbook for Biostatistics was a thick blue book by an Indian author with a long name, printed on paper so thin you could see the next page through it. I had owned it for five months. I had opened it, I estimated, perhaps eight times. I took it out of my bag that night and put it on the little table in the corner of the room I shared with nobody now, since my sisters had gone, and I looked at it.

I didn't open it. Not that night. That night I opened the diary.

I wrote for three hours. I have the pages still. They're not pretty. I wrote about the red R and about the noticeboard and about Sneha's three colours. I wrote about Rohan's laptop and the rasgullas in the cardboard box and the silence on the balcony when I'd said "biotechnology." I wrote about Mrs. Das. I had never written about Mrs. Das before, not properly, not in eleven years; I had written that first line, I am not stupid, and then never gone back to the room where it happened. That night I went back. I wrote the whole scene, the chalk, the forty-three heads turning, "Are you stupid," the laugh, the boy who muttered it behind me like he'd found a coin. I wrote what I had meant to say, and what I had said, and what she had heard.

And then I wrote about the neem by the school boundary wall, the one that had survived the cyclone. The peon and his steel lunch box. Roots go down looking. Lazy roots get pulled out.

And I wrote, at about two in the morning, with my hand aching:

What if I am not lazy. What if I am a summer plant that everyone keeps waiting to grow in the monsoon.

I looked at that for a long time. It was a strange sentence. I didn't really know what I meant by it. But I could feel that it was true in some way I hadn't yet worked out, the way you can feel a ball is going to swing before it leaves your hand.

* * *

What I worked out over the next few weeks, slowly, and mostly by writing, was something very simple. It's so simple that I'm embarrassed to say it. But nobody had ever told me, and I had to find it on my own, and I think there must be other people like me who also have to find it on their own, so I'll say it plainly.

My body was built to move. It had always been. The horse inside me, the old man at the dhaba near Angul had called it. The legs that kicked in the heat on the floor of Quarter No. 8. The boy who bowled every ball. The boy who stood up in the cyclone because he couldn't sit through it. I had spent my whole life trying to be still like the others, and failing, and calling the failure laziness. But the stillness the others had, Sneha and Rohan and the sixty heads in the tuition room, wasn't something they had and I didn't. It was something that came easily to their bodies and didn't come easily to mine.

That didn't mean I couldn't have it. It meant I had to build it. Like the roots of the neem. Not given by the rain; made, every day, in the heat, by someone carrying water.

And the other thing I worked out was this: the slide, the moment when my mind went sideways off the problem and out the window, wasn't the end. I had always treated it as the end. The moment I felt it, I gave up. I looked up at the clock, or the crow, or the girl with the pencil in her hair, and I thought, there it goes, I've lost it again, I'm lazy, and I stopped. But what if the slide was just the place where it got hard? What if it was the moment the batsman steps out of his crease and you have to decide whether to bowl the next ball anyway?

What if, at the slide, I just didn't stop?

* * *

The first night I tried it, it was awful.

I sat down at the little table at nine with the blue Biostatistics book and a notebook and a pencil. I had decided on one problem. Just one. A problem from the end of chapter four about confidence intervals, which I had looked at once in September and closed the book on. I read it. I read it again. I started to work it, and got through two lines, and then I felt it come: the slide, the softening, the drift.

My legs started to go. My eyes went to the window. There was nothing to see out of the window except the wall of the building opposite, but my eyes went there anyway, as if there might be something.

I made myself look back at the problem.

It was physically painful. I don't know how to describe it to someone whose mind doesn't do this. It was like holding a heavy bucket out at arm's length. Every part of me wanted to put it down. I could feel the muscles in my neck and shoulders tighten. My leg was bouncing so hard under the table that the pencil rolled off onto the floor.

I picked it up. I looked at the problem.

I didn't understand it. That was fine. I didn't need to understand it yet. I just needed to keep looking. So I looked. I read the first line again, one word at a time, very slowly, the way Chiku had once carried her little plastic jug up four floors with her tongue between her teeth. I wrote the first line out again in my own handwriting. And the second. I got to the place where I had lost it before, and lost it again, and went back and read the line before, and wrote it again.

At eleven I got up and walked around the room ten times. Not out of the flat. Just around the room. Then I sat down again.

At one in the morning, I still hadn't solved it. I had filled four pages with crossings-out. I had walked around the room maybe fifty times. My mother had got up once to use the bathroom and seen the light under my door and said nothing, and I'd heard her go back to bed.

At about ten to two, it opened.

I don't know what else to call it. It opened, the way a door that's been stuck for years suddenly gives when you've stopped expecting it to. I was staring at a formula on the page, the same formula I had been staring at for an hour, and suddenly I could see what it meant. Not just what to do with it. What it meant. Why it was shaped the way it was. Why the square root was there and what the little n underneath it was doing, and why it had to be there, and what would happen if it wasn't. I saw the whole thing at once, like seeing the colony from the balcony after you've only ever seen it from the road.

I solved the problem in about four minutes.

Then I sat there, at two in the morning, with my hand on the page, and I wasn't happy, exactly. I was too tired to be happy. I was something closer to frightened. Because if this was what had been on the other side of the slide all along, then I had spent eleven years walking away from it.

I opened the diary and wrote one line.

I sat through it. That's all it was.

* * *

I did it again the next night. And the night after that.

It got easier. Not quickly. It was like the water in the cyclone weeks; on the first day the bucket nearly broke my arms, and on the tenth day I noticed I had climbed two flights without stopping. I learned things about how I worked. I learned that I had to run in the morning before I could sit at night, a proper run, five or six kilometres, until the horse was tired. I learned that I could sit for about forty-five minutes before the slide came, and that when it came I had to stay for another ten or fifteen minutes no matter what, and that if I stayed, it usually opened. I learned that if I got up after that and walked around the room, ten times, and drank a glass of water, and sat down again, I could do another forty-five. I learned to write down, in the diary, every night, exactly where I had felt the slide and what had happened when I'd stayed through it, so that I could see the pattern.

There was a pattern. There was always a pattern. I was, it turned out, very good at seeing patterns, once I sat still long enough for them to show up.

I learned the thing nobody had told me. That the people who seemed to sit easily weren't better than me. Many of them, I would find out years later, had simply never been pushed to the place where it opened. They had sat through the easy parts and stood up at the hard ones, the same as me, only they could sit through more of the easy parts, so it looked like discipline. Some of them never found the door at all. They went through life on easy marks and never knew what was on the other side of the hard ones.

I knew. Now I knew.

* * *

In July 2008 the rains came early and heavy, the way they used to in the old days, and on the morning of my supplementary Biostatistics exam it had been raining for two days. I took the bus to the college in a plastic raincoat with my admit card in a polythene bag. The road past Patia was flooded to the ankles. The college's second floor still had no glass in some of its windows, and the rain blew in onto the corridor floor in grey sheets.

I sat at a desk in an exam hall with sixteen other re-appear students, and the invigilator handed out the paper, and I read it.

I felt the slide at about forty minutes. Right on schedule. The paper went soft. The question in front of me looked back at me like a stranger.

I stayed.

I stayed for ten minutes, staring at it, my leg bouncing under the desk, my hands cold. I didn't look at the window. I didn't look at the clock. I read the question again, one word at a time, the way you carry a full bucket. And it opened.

I got eighty-one.

When I saw the mark on the noticeboard, I didn't shout. I didn't run. I stood there with the rain blowing in through the glassless window behind me and I read it three times, the way my father read every mark on my report cards, slowly, top to bottom, checking it for errors. There were no errors.

I went home. My father was on the balcony with the Samaja. I told him.

He put down the paper.

He looked at me for a long moment, his face doing nothing, the way it always did nothing. Then he said, "Eighty-one."

"Eighty-one."

He nodded. It was the nod. The one he'd given me at the top of the stairs in Quarter No. 8 when I was nine years old and had knocked one brick off a stack in an empty road in the dark, after fifty balls.

"Hit it," he said.

"Once," I said.

And then my father, who never laughed, laughed. A short surprised bark, as if it had come out of him without permission. And I laughed too, and my mother came out of the kitchen with her hands wet and said, "What? What is it?" and neither of us could tell her.

My final year marks were the best I'd ever had. Not the best in the batch. Sneha was still first. But I was seventh, out of thirty-eight, and my final-year lab project, on tissue culture of a medicinal plant from the western Odisha forests, got the highest mark in the department. Sahu, the old lab assistant, shook my hand at the end of the viva and said, "I told you. There is everything there."

He hadn't told me that. I had told him. But I let him have it.

Chapter 13

The Hard End of the Pitch

Nobody hired biotechnologists in 2008. It was the year the banks in America fell over, and somehow, in a way I never fully understood, that meant the pharmaceutical company in Hyderabad that had shortlisted me for a junior research post sent a two-line email in October saying the position had been "put on hold due to market conditions." The seed company in Pune never replied at all. The state government research institute in Bhubaneswar had a vacancy, one, for which four hundred people applied.

I had my degree. I had my eighty-one and my seventh rank and my lab project. I had learned to sit. And I had no job.

The walks came back, briefly, that winter. Not as bad. I knew how to sit now, and I knew that the walks were not the same as thinking, and so I used them differently; I walked to tire out the horse and then came home and sat and wrote out, carefully, every option I had, with the costs and the odds, the way my father would have checked a survey sheet. It was a short list. Most of it was crossed out.

At the bottom of the list, almost as an afterthought, was a line I had copied from a newspaper advertisement. A big technology company, one of the American ones, was hiring for its support centre in Bangalore. Night shifts. Any graduate. Good English required. Training provided.

I looked at that line for a long time.

It was not biotechnology. It was not research. It was not, in any sense anyone in Survey of India Colony would have recognised, a career. It was a call centre. That was what people called it. I could already see the tilt of Bada Bapa's head.

I applied.

* * *

I took the Konark Express to Bangalore in February 2009, twenty-six hours, with a trolley bag that had belonged to Mamuni Nani's husband and a steel tiffin carrier of my mother's chhena poda that I finished before Vijayawada. My father came to the station. He stood on the platform with his hands behind his back while I found my berth, and when the train started to move he walked alongside the window for a few steps, and then he stopped and raised two fingers, the old salute, and I raised two fingers back, and then he was gone behind a pillar.

Bangalore in 2009 was cool and green and confused, a city that had grown too fast and hadn't caught up with itself. It rained for an hour in the afternoons and stopped, politely, as if embarrassed. I found it very strange. I shared a two-bedroom flat in a place called BTM Layout with four other boys from the training batch: two from Kerala, one from Bihar, one from Assam. We cooked rice in a rice cooker and ate it with pickle from five different states and argued about cricket in English, which was the only language we all had.

The work was this. Big companies, businesses, used the software that my employer made. When it broke, or when they couldn't make it do what they wanted, they called. Someone had to pick up the phone. That someone, at three in the morning Bangalore time, was me.

The training lasted six weeks. Then they put me on a floor with two hundred other people in headsets, rows and rows of them under white lights, and I picked up my first call, and a man in Ohio shouted at me for eleven minutes because his email server was down and his company was losing money every second it was down and did I have any idea, any idea at all.

I didn't. I put him on hold, which you were not supposed to do, and asked the team lead, who sighed and showed me a screen I hadn't been trained on. I took the man off hold. I fixed it. It took forty minutes. At the end he said, grudgingly, "Okay. Thanks," and hung up.

I sat there with the headset on, my hands shaking slightly.

Then the phone rang again.

* * *

There is a thing that happens on every support floor in the world, and it happened on ours in the first month. The calls sort themselves. The easy ones, password resets and licence questions and people who have forgotten to plug something in, are quick and pleasant and make your numbers look good, because you are measured on how many you close and how fast. The hard ones are slow, and ugly, and full of angry people, and you can spend six hours on a single one and close nothing, and your numbers look terrible.

Everyone wanted the easy calls. Everyone avoided the hard ones. There were tricks for it; you learned them in the first two weeks from the people who had been there longer. You could be "on a break" when you saw a certain kind of ticket come into the queue. You could transfer a call to another team before it got complicated. You could, if you were very good at it, make sure that the hard ones always landed on somebody else.

I didn't do any of that. I took the hard ones.

I want to be clear that I didn't do it because I was a good person. I did it for the same reason I bowled. I had worked out, on the first night of the man from Ohio, that the easy calls weren't going to teach me anything. I could close a thousand password resets and still know exactly what I had known on day one. The hard ones were where the system actually showed you how it worked, how it broke, why. The hard ones were where you had to sit.

So I sat. Night after night. Six hours on a single ticket, sometimes, with my legs bouncing under the desk and the slide coming at forty-five minutes and me staying through it, staying, staying, until the problem opened. I kept a notebook beside my keyboard, a fresh diary, and wrote down every hard problem I'd solved and how, so that the next time I would know. By the end of the first year it was three notebooks. By the end of the second it was six.

My numbers were bad, the first few months. My team lead, a kind tired man from Mangalore, called me in and said, "Max, you're closing half what the others close. What's going on?"

"I'm taking the hard ones."

"Why?"

"Someone has to," I said. And then, because it was true: "And I like it."

He looked at me the way Rohan used to look at me on the road outside Block C. Irritation and reluctant interest.

"You're mad," he said.

"I know."

He left me alone. Six months later, when the hard tickets started coming to me by name, because the customers had started asking for me by name, he put me up for a promotion.

* * *

I'll go through the next years quickly, because they're the part of the story that's least interesting to tell and was the hardest to live.

I was promoted. Then promoted again. I moved from the phones to a team that handled only the worst problems, the ones that came from the biggest customers, the banks and the airlines and the governments, where a broken system could mean a hundred thousand people unable to do their jobs. I moved from Bangalore to Hyderabad and back to Bangalore. I learned to speak in meetings. This was harder than anything I'd ever done, harder than Biostatistics; the boy who had spoken to two people in the whole of Survey of India Colony had to stand in a room of strangers, some of them in suits flown in from Seattle, and say "This is what went wrong, and this is what we're going to do." I wrote what I was going to say in the diary the night before, every time, and practised it on the balcony of whatever flat I was living in, to the wall opposite.

Somewhere around year six I stopped being the person who fixed things and became the person who looked after the people who fixed things. Then the person who looked after the customers who were too big for anyone else to look after. Contracts with numbers in them I would not have been able to say out loud in my father's flat without someone asking me to repeat it. I flew to places I had only seen on the cards in the Gold Flake tin. Sydney, where I went to the Sydney Cricket Ground on a Sunday and stood in the empty stands and thought about Rohan. London. Seattle, where it rained the Bangalore way, briefly and politely, and I missed Bhubaneswar so much I went back to the hotel and called my mother.

I took the hard job every time. Every escalation no one wanted. Every customer who had made three other managers quit. Every night call from a different time zone. It became, in the end, what I was known for; people would say, in meetings I wasn't in, I was told later, "Give it to Max, he'll sit with it." I liked that. I liked it more than any title they gave me.

My father never once said he was proud of me.

* * *

I want to be fair to him. He came to visit me in Bangalore in 2014, with my mother, and I took them to see the office, the big glass building with the security gates and the cafeteria with food from every state in India. My mother walked around it with her mouth slightly open, touching things. My father walked behind her with his hands behind his back, looking at everything, the way he had looked at the specification for a government building in 1999, if he had ever seen it.

In the cafeteria he said, "How many people work here?"

"In this building, about four thousand."

"And you are in charge of how many?"

"Directly? About sixty. Indirectly, more."

He nodded. He ate a dosa from left to right.

On the way out, at the security gate, he stopped and looked back up at the glass building, at the logo on the top of it, for a long moment.

"This is not biotechnology," he said.

"No, Bapa."

"Hm," he said. And that was all.

I lay awake that night in my flat with my parents asleep in my bedroom and me on the sofa, and I felt eleven years old. I felt like I had carried twelve buckets up four floors and he had looked at the drums and said, "Hm."

I wrote in the diary: Twenty-six years old. Still waiting for Bapa to say it.

And then, under it, because I had learned by then that the diary was where I had to be honest even when I didn't want to be: Maybe he never will. Maybe that's his language. Maybe "hm" is the whole sentence.

I didn't believe that. Not yet.

* * *

Rohan, for what it's worth, had his own road.

He went from his engineering college in Rourkela to a software company in Pune, then to one in Gurgaon, then to an MBA, then to a bank in Singapore, where he did something with money that he explained to me once over a long phone call and that I still don't understand. He married a girl from Cuttack whose mother had once been Lipi Nani's teacher. He had two children, both girls, both of whom he taught to bat in a Singapore condominium car park with a tennis ball and a set of plastic stumps.

He called me at two in the morning one night in 2017, my time, after a bad day, and talked for an hour about nothing, about cricket and old cards and the footprint on his bat, and then, near the end, he said, "Do you remember when you used to bowl and bowl and I used to just bat?"

"I remember."

"I used to think you were the biggest idiot in Bhubaneswar."

"I know. You told me. Every day."

He laughed. Then he was quiet for a long time, so long I thought the line had dropped.

"I think about that," he said. "I think I got good at standing there. And you got good at running in." He didn't say anything else. I didn't need him to.

"You still have the bat?" I said.

"The blade. In a cupboard."

"Keep it."

"Obviously, re."

* * *

In 2021, at thirty-three, I left.

It surprised everyone. I had a good job, a big title, a flat in Bangalore with a balcony that looked out on a rain tree. My mother cried on the phone. My father said, "Hm." Mamuni Nani told me, in her secretariat voice, that I was being irresponsible. Lipi Nani asked if I was sick.

Only Chiku, who was a doctor by then, in Delhi, and the only one in the family who had always known exactly what she wanted, said, "Bhaaai. Finally." In the long rising voice she had used when she was four, calling a cow in from a field.

I had seen something, in those last years at the big company. I had seen that the machines were learning to talk. Not like the old software, which did only what you told it and broke when you asked it anything else. These new systems could read and write and reason, more or less. They could answer the easy calls. Soon, I could see, they would answer most of the hard ones too.

The people around me were afraid of it. I understood that. But I sat with it, the way I sat with everything, and after a while it opened, and what I saw on the other side was not a threat. I saw a tool for people like me. For boys at the back of the assembly. For the ones with horses inside them, who couldn't sit through the lecture but could sit through a problem if someone would only let them come at it their own way. For the people on the support floor at three in the morning, drowning in tickets.

I started building. Small things at first, with two friends from the old job, in a rented room above a bakery in Indiranagar that smelled of cardamom buns all day. I wrote code, badly at first, the coding Rohan had told me to learn on his laptop in 2006, sixteen years late. I sat through it. It opened.

It was the hardest thing I had ever done. Every week something broke. Every month we nearly ran out of money. There was nobody to escalate to; I was the escalation. There was no one to give the hard job to.

I loved it more than anything I'd done in my life.

Chapter 14

Summer Planting

The letter came to my parents' flat in Nayapalli in March of last year, and my mother read it to me over the phone in the voice she used for official things, which was the voice of a woman reading out a ration card.

"They are writing to all the old allottees," my mother said. "In case anyone wants to collect anything. As if we left anything." She paused. "Your father read it twice."

"What did he say?"

"He said, 'Good steel. They used good steel in those days.' And then he went for his walk."

I flew to Bhubaneswar on the second of April.

Bhubaneswar had become a city that had flyovers and malls and a cricket stadium where international matches were played, and a stretch along Jaydev Vihar where the restaurants had English names and valet parking. Thirty-nine degrees in the first week of April, and the loo coming off the tarmac in waves, and the old feeling of my shirt sticking to my back before I had walked from the terminal to the car. I had not spent a summer in Bhubaneswar since 2008. I had forgotten how the heat had a personality. It met me at the door like a relative who had been waiting, and was a little offended that I had been away so long.

My father was seventy-two. He had become, in the last few years, a smaller version of himself, the way old men do, as if he were being slowly folded for storage. His knee was worse. He walked with a stick now, a plain wooden one that he hated and leaned on anyway. "It can run without me for a while. I've built it so it can." This was mostly true. I had two very good people who had told me, in almost exactly these words, to go away and stop hovering. "I can work from here. Laptop."

"Hm," said my father. He went back to the Samaja.

I went into the bedroom to unpack. Under my clothes, in a plastic bag that had once held a sari, were the diaries. All of them. Twenty-nine, at last count, from the thin one with the film actress on the cover to a leather one with my company's logo on it that someone had given me at a conference. I had brought them all. I didn't know why, exactly. It had just seemed like the right time for them to come home.

* * *

The estate officer at the colony office was a young man in his late twenties with a moustache he was clearly still deciding about. The office had been rebuilt after the cyclone, a squat pink building, and was going to be demolished too, in a later phase. On the wall behind his desk was a framed photograph of the colony from the air, taken in what looked like the early eighties, and I stood and looked at it for so long that he cleared his throat.

"You want to plant a tree," he said, reading the note I had written.

"Near Block C. In the strip between the road and the colony wall. Where the new towers won't go." I had looked at the redevelopment plan, which was pinned to the noticeboard outside. "There's going to be a garden there, according to the plan. A green belt. I'd like to plant one tree in it now. I'll look after it. I'll pay for it."

He looked at me, and then at the note, and then at me.

"Sir," he said, politely, "it is April."

"I know."

"It is thirty-nine degrees. It will be forty-four by May. You will have to water it every day. Twice a day." He spread his hands, reasonable. "If you wait until the monsoon, end of June, you can plant it then and the rain will do everything. Everybody plants in the monsoon. Van Mahotsav is in July. We are doing a whole drive. You can plant with the drive."

I smiled at him. I couldn't help it. I think he thought I was mad.

"I'd like to plant it now," I said. "I'll water it. Every day."

He looked at me for a while longer, and then shrugged, and signed the note, and stamped it with a rubber stamp that he had to breathe on twice before it worked.

"Your wish, sir."

On the way out, I paused at the door. "Is the tube well still there? Behind Block F?"

He looked surprised. "Yes. It's still working. How do you know about that?"

"I carried water from it once."

* * *

I bought the sapling from a nursery near Khandagiri, where a woman with betel-stained teeth and a Bluetooth earpiece walked me up and down rows of black polythene bags until I found what I wanted. A mango. A local one, not a grafted hybrid, about two feet tall, with a dozen dark leaves and a stem as thick as my thumb.

"For the garden?" she said.

"For the colony."

"Plant in July," she said, with total certainty, putting it into the auto for me. "Now it will burn."

"I'll water it."

She looked at me the way everybody had always looked at me. "Your wish," she said.

* * *

I planted it on the morning of the eighth of April, at six, before the sun had climbed over the RRL trees.

The strip of ground between Block C and the colony wall was hard as a floor. Dry red earth, packed, with tufts of dead grass and a crushed Frooti carton and a piece of blue plastic from somebody's old school bag. I had brought a crowbar and a shovel from a hardware shop in Unit 2, and I dug a hole about two feet deep and two feet across, and it took me an hour, and by the end of it my shirt was black with sweat and my hands were raw. I filled the bottom of the hole with compost from the nursery and some water, and set the sapling in it, and cut away the polythene bag with my mother's tailoring scissors, which I had borrowed without telling her, again, thirty years later.

Then I pressed the earth in around it with my hands.

I was on my knees in the dirt, patting the soil down, when I heard the tap of the stick behind me.

My father was standing at the edge of the road in his white kurta, leaning on the stick. I had not heard him come. He must have taken an auto from Nayapalli, at six in the morning, without telling anyone. My mother told me later he had got up at four-thirty and sat dressed on the balcony for an hour, waiting for it to be light enough.

"You did not wait," he said.

"No."

He looked at the sapling for a long time. Then he looked up at the block behind it. Block C, Type II. Four floors. The balcony at the top on the left, Quarter No. 8, with a new family's washing on the line, the last family who would ever live there.

"Hm," he said.

He lowered himself, slowly, painfully, onto the low wall at the edge of the road, the same wall where we had sat as boys trading Center Fresh cards, with a grey archaeological layer of gum still stuck under it, I was almost sure. He put the stick across his knees.

"Water," he said.

"I'm going to get it now."

"From the tube well?"

"Yes."

"I will pump," he said.

"Bapa, your knee..."

"I will pump," he said again, in the voice he had used for the specification of a government building. "You carry."

We did it every day. For eleven weeks.

Every morning at six, and every evening at six, through the whole of the summer. I would pick him up from Nayapalli in an auto, or later, when the auto driver got used to us, the auto would just come, and we would go to the colony, and walk, slowly, with his stick, to the tube well behind Block F. He would pump. I would hold the bucket. Then I would carry it, two buckets each trip, across the colony and around Block D to the strip of ground by Block C, and pour them slowly around the base of the mango, and watch the dry red earth drink, and go back.

Eight buckets in the morning. Eight in the evening. My father kept count in a small notebook he carried in his kurta pocket.

The residents watched us. Of course they did. It was Survey of India Colony. The families who were still there, waiting for their shifting date, stood on balconies in the evenings and called to each other across the gap between buildings. "That is the old man from Quarter No. 8. And his son. The one who went to Bangalore." "What are they doing?" "Watering a tree." "In May?" "In May." "Why?" Somebody, I never found out who, started putting out a plastic chair for my father by the tube well, every morning, so that he could sit and pump. Then a cushion appeared on the chair.

The heat came on. By the end of April it was forty-two. By the middle of May it was forty-four, and the radio was reading out advisories again, and the roads at noon were empty, and the onion-in-the-pocket was still, I discovered, a thing that mothers in Bhubaneswar insisted on. The mango sapling drooped in the afternoons. Some days its leaves curled at the edges and went pale, and I would stand over it in the evening, sweating, and feel the old fear, the mango in the Dalda tin, the brown stick in October.

Then I would water it, and in the morning it would be standing up again.

By the middle of May it had put out new leaves at the tip. They were the colour of copper, soft and limp, the way new mango leaves are, like something not yet sure of itself. My father leaned on his stick and looked at them for a long time.

"Copper," he said.

"Yes."

"In the village we used to say, copper leaves mean the roots have found something."

He was forty pounds heavier than the boy on the road outside Block C. He had grey at his temples and an expensive watch and an accent that had picked up something Singaporean in the vowels. He had brought the bat.

Not the whole bat. The blade, the old cheap blade from the sports shop at Unit 2, cracked down the middle and bound with insulating tape, with the footprint still on it. On the road between Block C and Block D, at six in the evening, in the heat, with the electric pole and a stack of three bricks I'd taken from the demolition pile for wickets, and a tennis ball I'd bought from the shop at the corner of the main road, the one where I used to buy Pepsi bottles in a cloth bag. Rohan's daughters stood at the drain and fielded, screaming. His wife filmed it on her phone. "Bowl properly, re!" shouted Rohan.

"To the colony. To check on it."

"Yes."

We went in an auto whose driver was either very brave or very greedy, through streets full of broken branches, with the rain coming in through the open sides and soaking us both. At the colony gate we got out and walked, my father with his stick and my arm, me with the torch, the wind shoving at us, the rain loud on the tin roofs of the sheds, the dark full of noise.

The mango was flat.

My heart stopped. It was bent over almost to the ground, its leaves pressed into the mud, the wind holding it down like a hand.

Then the wind dropped for a second, and it came up.

It came up slowly, shaking off the water, and stood, swaying. And then the wind came again and pushed it down, and it went down, and the wind dropped, and it came up again.

We stood there in the rain and watched it. Down. Up. Down. Up. Every time the wind let go, it stood.

My father's hand was on my shoulder. I don't know when he had put it there. It was heavy and warm and shaking slightly, from cold or from age or from something else.

He leaned close to my ear so I could hear him over the noise.

"Roots," he said.

"Yes."

"Summer roots."

"Yes, Bapa."

We stood there for a long time, until the wind went down and the rain began to fall properly, downwards, the way it was supposed to. Then I took him home.

* * *

The monsoon arrived four days later, on schedule, on the twenty-seventh of June. Heavy and slow and enormous, off the Bay of Bengal, the way it always had. It rained for three days without stopping. Drops like small stones. The roads turned to rivers. I lay awake at night in the flat in Nayapalli, listening to it on the roof, and I did not do any sums about drowning.

On the third morning, it eased, and I went to the colony with my father.

The mango was standing in a pool of brown water, every leaf shining, the copper ones at the top already turning green. The ground around it was soft and black. All along the colony wall, along the strip where the green belt would go, there were new things growing: grass, weeds, little bright seedlings of everything, coming up out of the mud by themselves, in their hundreds, the way things do in Odisha in the rain.

The mango stood a head above all of them.

"Now you don't have to water," said my father.

"No."

"The rain will do it."

"The rain is extra," I said.

He turned and looked at me. It took him a long time, with the stick. He looked at my face as if he were checking it against a survey sheet.

Then he took the small notebook out of his kurta pocket. Not this summer's notebook. An older one. Its cover was soft and grey and torn at the corner. He opened it carefully, his hands shaking a little, and held it out to me.

It was dated November 1999.

It was a list of days. One line for each, in his careful hand, with the seven that had a line through it. Water. Kerosene. Rice. And next to each day, under a column headed simply M., a number. The number of buckets I had carried up four floors.

Agreed 7, it said at the top of the column, in pencil, underlined.

Underneath, day after day after day, for five weeks: 8. 8. 9. 8. 10. 8. 9.

Never seven. Not once.

I looked up at him. I couldn't speak.

"I kept it," my father said. "In the almirah. In the drawer."

The special drawer.

He took the notebook back from me and closed it and put it in his pocket. He looked at the tree. He was quiet for a long time, leaning on his stick, the rain starting again lightly, falling on both of us.

"I used to say to you," he said, "next time."

"I remember."

"I was wrong." He said it simply, the way he said everything, as if it were a number that had finally checked out. "It was always this time. You were always doing it this time. I just did not know how to measure it."

He put his hand on my head, the way he used to when I was small, for a second, and took it away.

"You were right," he said. "About summer."

* * *

They brought Block C down on the second of July. I didn't watch. I've been told it took most of a day, and that the steel inside was very good, and that the machine operator had trouble with it.

The mango is still there. My mother sends me a photo every month on WhatsApp, always from the same angle, always slightly blurred, with no message. It is taller than me now. The new towers are going up behind it, eight floors, with lifts. The children who will live in them will have a tree in the green belt that someone planted in the wrong season, and nobody will remember who, and that is fine. That was always fine.

I went back to Bangalore at the end of July, to the room above the bakery that we've long since outgrown, to the work, to the hard calls nobody else wants. I still run in the mornings. I still sit at night. I still feel the slide come at forty-five minutes, every time, regular as a train. I still stay.

The night I got back, I sat on the balcony of my flat, the one with the rain tree, with the first diary and the last one side by side on my knees. It was raining. Bangalore rain, polite, brief, already stopping.

I opened the last diary to a clean page. I thought for a long time about what to write. Some kind of ending. Something that would tie it all together, the boy and the bricks and the buckets and the tree.

In the end I only wrote one line.

Watered it. It held.
